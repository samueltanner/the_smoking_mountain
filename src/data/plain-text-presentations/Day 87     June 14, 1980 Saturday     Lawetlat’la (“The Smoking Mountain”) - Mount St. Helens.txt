Spirit Lake  Part I     Pre-Eruption & May 18 effects

Revisiting Pre-Eruption Spirit Lake
Spirit Lake was formed 3900 to 3300 years ago when the upper North Fork Toutle River basin was dammed by a broad lahar fan and pyroclastic deposits during the voluminous Smith Creek eruptive period. The next photograph clearly shows how the fan of deposits dammed the valley and created the ancestral lake. It is unknown if the lake existed before that time.
Spirit Lake was dammed again during the Pine Creek eruptive period (2900-2500 years ago). The lake filled and breached these debris dams several times, sending massive lahars down the Toutle valley. In the summers of 1938 and 1939, botanist Donald B. Lawrence took cores in Douglas Firs on Spirit Lake’s south shore and measured their growth rings. Among the mature trees were cylindrical wells three feet wide and fifteen feet deep. Drowned in volcanic debris, the old trees had rotted out and left behind the deep tree wells. Lawrence dated that debris avalanche to about 1550 A.D. and estimated the flow dammed Spirit Lake and raised the water surface level sixty feet. Lawrence’s early Spirit Lake research is a great read.
Spirit Lake had been sounded in the past by Donal Mullineaux, Rocky Crandell and others. Their measurements, from shoal on the southern shore to deep water in both arms, helped confirm botanist Lawrence’s 1938-39 study finding Spirit Lake had been dammed by prehistoric eruptions. Despite the soundings, Spirit Lake had never been completely mapped before 1974 and chemical analyses were few.

Leonard Nelson, Norm Dion, and Gil Bortelson performed a water chemistry analysis and bathymetric mapping of Spirit Lake in fall 1974, along with Jim Innes and Jim McConnell from the Tacoma office. 
Gil Bortelson recently described the methodology used for the initial 1974 Spirit Lake bathymetry…
“Large-scale vertical aerial photographs were used to locate cross-sectional data for lake depth. We had as a protocol a minimum number of x-sections to produce the bathymetry. But many more sections were needed if the lake had an irregular shoreline, like Spirit Lake’s horseshoe configuration. Len Nelson was the team expert in charge of field producing the data except if a helicopter was used, in the instance of inaccessible lakes, mostly alpine. The boat or helicopter travelled at constant speed, normally determined by a current meter. The cross-sectional data was digitized in our Tacoma office and that data was sent to Menlo Park where they had the  capability to use a computerized program to produce the bathymetric maps. The fathometer was made by Raytheon. I do not know the model, but likely the high end for the era. The Menlo Park program also computed physical data such as lake volume, mean depth, shoreline length, etc.” 

Spirit Lake had a pre-eruption water surface level of 3198 feet. After mapping, the big lake was found to have a maximum depth of 190 feet in the East Arm and 120-foot depth in the West Arm. Spirit Lake had a 1,300-acre surface area, 160,000-acre-foot volume, and water clarity to 36 feet in depth (Secchi-disk measurement).  


The Radically Altered Spirit Lake      
When the earthquake-triggered debris avalanche roared down Loo-wit’s northern slope on Sunday morning May 18, Slide Block I was deposited first and instantly blocked Spirit Lake’s outlet. Parts of the block slid into Spirit Lake, flowed up and over 1300-foot high South Coldwater Ridge, or raced down the North Fork Toutle River valley. The parts of Slide Block I were named the Spirit Lake geologic unit, the Coldwater Ridge (Johnston) unit, and the portion traveling down the North Fork Toutle was termed the Marginal unit.
A short sequence of events at Spirit Lake in the second, third, and fourth minute after the initial earthquake (0832.2 hours)…
• The front of the pyroclastic density current reached Spirit Lake at approximately 0833.5, swept northeast across Spirit Lake basin at roughly 250 mph, leveled the mature coniferous forest and deposited gravel layer A1. 
• The toe of the the debris avalanche entered the lake at about 155 mph (0834 hours) and plowed northward along both the West and East arms of Spirit Lake.
• As part of the debris avalanche climbed and topped South Coldwater Ridge, part of the landslide dropped east
off Harry’s Ridge into the lake. [see photograph †1]
• A giant wave created by the debris avalanche rushed 860 feet up the steep lakeshore valleys, crashed across the Eagle Point peninsula from the West Arm into the East Arm (0834.5-0835.5) [see photograph †2], and raced up into Harmony Falls basin before the water ran back into the lakebed.    
• The continuing pyroclastic density current (lateral pyroclastic surge) deposited sandy layer A2 and gradually waned. Silt layer A3 fell 30–90 minutes later, partly from lingering low dust but mainly from the Plinian column.

The Spirit Lake geologic unit instantly filled the lake with 555 million cubic yards of volcanic material, 17 percent of the massive 3.3 billion cubic yard landslide. The avalanche buried Spirit Lake’s former lakebed under debris 320 feet thick in the East Arm and 240-280 feet thick in the West Arm (†3 map). The displaced lake water was pushed up the facing mountainsides toward the Mount Margaret massif, The Dome, and Norway Pass. The water stripped the slopes down to bedrock, and on its return carried the forest and most of its soil back into the lakebed. The wave also erased the just deposited coarse basal layer A1 of the lateral surge up to 4870 feet in elevation. Mountain slopes all around the basin were also denuded by the sloshing lake, and the oscillating water left clumps of shattered and pyrolyzed trees on high terraces and created huge erosion gullies. Spirit Lake's water surface level was raised 206 feet in elevation (3198' to 3404'), with the initial measurement of the new water level taken by an altimeter in a hovering helicopter. The water surface level receded in the first few dry summer months after the eruption since the snowpack was gone and tributary streams were primarily dry (†4).  
Spirit Lake was originally thought to have vanished in the eruption.  Airborne radar did not detect the usual reflection of the lake’s water due to the mass of floating logs on the surface.

The debris avalanche deposited large numbers of hummocks of wildly varying colors into the West Arm of Spirit Lake, and for a half-mile up Bear Creek above the new lake level. As Spirit Lake’s displaced water rushed back downslope and sloshed, it eroded the hummocks into sharp conical shapes. Logs were stranded on top of hummocks, and deep box canyons were cut between them. Many of the hummocks slowly submerged as the lake water rose, but many others are still visible in Bear Cove. Logs are also grounded on top of several of the barely submerged hummocks.

Spirit Lake’s volume, surface area, surface water level, and depth were completely altered after being inundated by the debris avalanche...essentially creating a new lake. The lake’s surface area increased substantially from 1300 acres to 2200 acres. The length of the shoreline doubled. The maximum depth in the East Arm had changed from 190 feet pre-eruption to only 80 feet post-eruption. Spirit Lake lost 37,200 acre-feet in volume during the eruption (from 160,000 af to 122,800 af) and continued to lose volume during the summer months and early fall 1980. Then the November rains began in earnest.
