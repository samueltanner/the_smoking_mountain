Day 9     March 28, 1980  Friday
USGS daily report
Another explosive eruption began about 0300 hours today and lasted nearly two hours. Although observations were hampered by darkness, a cloud of ash and steam was seen by airborne observers to rise "more than a mile" above the volcano, and an ash cloud cascaded slowly down its eastern flank. By dusk, at least a dozen other eruptions lasting from a few minutes to nearly an hour had occurred. Columns of steam and ash at times reached nearly 10,000 feet above the volcano and drifted eastward. An eruption just before sunset threw out not only ash but also rocks that ranged in size from a few inches to about three feet across. A short time later, volcanic ash was falling in a wide swath east of the volcano. It was reported as far away as Trout Lake, about 35 miles to the southeast. Several mud-darkened avalanches of snow and ice moved down the eastern flank of the mountain; a few extended down to the 6,400-foot level. Because of their dark color, some observers mistook them for lava flows or mudflows, an error that added to concerns about flood hazards in the valleys of streams draining the mountain. 
Earthquake activity remains about the same. Eight events of magnitude 4 or greater took place today. The strongest earthquake to date, which occurred shortly after midnight this morning, registered 4.9 in Seattle. Geophysicists report that the earthquakes are centered beneath the volcano at a depth of less than one mile. Scientists from the University of Washington Cloud Physics Research Group flew over the volcano to collect samples of the emanating gases. They found that sulfur dioxide, which is associated with high temperatures, was the main sulfur gas present. This discovery was the first indication that a high-temperature source inside the mountain is releasing gas during eruptions. The presence of sulfur dioxide in the gases is of special interest because its content is usually low in geothermal steam but relatively high in gases emanating from magma. Despite the changeable weather and frequent eruptions, scientists in a helicopter flew to the 4,000-foot level of the volcano to collect ash samples for analysis to determine whether the volcanic ash was coming from new magma or from pulverized old rock. The preceding photograph of David Johnston was taken by my cousin, Warren Anderson. Warren (Bud) was my father’s first cousin and best friend in their college and young adult times. Warren was a longtime photographer for the Tacoma News Tribune, and took Johnston’s photo at Timberline when David surprised reporters by saying they were in a very dangerous spot. Johnston famously said, “It’s like we’re standing on a powder keg and the fuse has been lit, but we don’t know how long it is.”
Jim Erickson, who wrote today’s Tacoma News Tribune articles about Mount St. Helens and many others, was Warren’s close friend and colleague. Jim wrote a book, Memories of Mount St. Helens, in 2020.

Historic photographs
I love the following historic photographs taken by Harry Treadway in 1930, when aerial photography was just beginning. Unbroken valleys and ridges of never-cut trees, and both Mount St. Helens and Mount Adams wearing a cloud cap in the first shot. 

In the mid-1930s the Regional Forester, Pacific Northwest Region, recognized the unusually high recreation value of the Mount St. Helens/Spirit Lake Area and gave the area a "Limited Area Classification". This classification was unique to Oregon and Washington, and essentially was a stop-look-and listen decision to preserve the status quo of the area until further study. 
There was little timber harvesting activity in this area before 1950. In the early 1950s private owners started harvesting their timber in the area north of Swift Reservoir. Following a serious Chermes epidemic in 1954-1957, The Forest Service and private owners commenced major harvest programs in the areas southwest, south and east of Mt. St. Helens. By the 1960s there were many harvest operations in the Smith, Clearwater, Quartz and upper Green River drainages. Most private owners liquidated the timber on their ownerships, so entire sections were clearcut. National forest timber was harvested on a staggered setting basis. No National Forest timber was cut in the Spirit Lake basin, Mount Margaret backcountry or the lower Green River prior to the eruption of Mount St. Helens. Weyerhaeuser land along the West forest boundary from the South Fork Toutle to the Green River was mostly harvested in the decades of the 1960s and 1970s.

In 1959 a Multiple Use Plan for the Spirit Lake Ranger District was approved by the Regional Forester. The Limited 

Area Classification was rescinded in recognition of the fact that the checker-board land ownership pattern precluded considering any of the area for wilderness status. Management direction in the plan gave priority to the high recreation values. No commercial timber sales were permitted within view of Spirit Lake. The Mount Margaret area was to continue as an unroaded backcountry recreation area.
- C. Glen Jorgensen  Gifford Pinchot Forest Supervisor (1957-1961)
