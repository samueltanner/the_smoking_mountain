Day 86     June 13, 1980  Friday

USGS daily report
The eruption that began last night apparently continued sporadically into the early hours of this morning. At 0202 hours, the Portland office of the National Weather Service reported that its radar showed "a heavy plume" up to 15,000 to 16,000. feet above sea level. The pilot of the Forest Service observation airplane reported at 0231 that, at his position 22 miles northwest of Pendleton, Oreg., he was "in ash" at an altitude of 24,000 feet and that the plane was being peppered with pumice particles as large as one-eighth inch in diameter. The plane outdistanced the eastward-moving ash and landed safely in Spokane after having been warned away from Portland area airports. At 0400 hours, the National Weather Service radar showed the plume top to be at 13,000 feet and the ash content to be slightly reduced. At 0600, the University of Washington seismology center reported that steady, low-level harmonic tremor was being recorded. 
Although last night's eruption was shorter than the one on May 25th (about five hours as opposed to more than 12 hours), it may have spewed more ash, according to USGS geologists. Vancouver received about one-eighth inch, and Portland received about one-sixteenth inch. The coastal city of Tillamook, Oregon, nearly 100 miles southwest of Mount St. Helens, apparently lay directly in the path of the plume that reached the Oregon coast. As much as one-eighth inch of ash reportedly fell on that city, but little or none fell on nearby communities to the north and south. The flash flood watch for the Toutle, Cowlitz, Kalama, and Lewis Rivers was cancelled. In the eight hours following last night's eruption, many small aftershocks were recorded at the University of Washington seismology center. Twenty-one of these shocks were strong enough that preliminary locations could be calculated. 

These quakes originated at depths of about four-seven miles below the surface and were located just west of the volcano's crater. Radar imagery obtained at about 1400 hours by the Oregon Army National Guard clearly shows what appears to be a dome in the position of the former inner crater. Helicopter reconnaissance around the mountain today could not verify the presence of the dome, but observers could see that the eruption had caused extensive new pumice flows north of the volcano and minor mudflows that ended on the mountain itself. The pumice flows had poured out of the open end of the amphitheater and rushed downslope to the north, completely burying the pyroclastic flow from the May 25th eruption and filling phreatic explosion craters that formed in the May 18th debris avalanche deposits. One lobe flowed nearly five miles, stopping within 30 yards of Spirit Lake. Another extended westward more than one mile in the upper part of the North Fork Toutle River valley. USGS geologist Peter Rowley said that the material ejected in the eruption was from fresh magma rather than old rock or ash from the larger May 18th crater. Temperatures measured at a depth of ten feet in the June 12th pyroclastic flows are more than 1,100°F (600°C) - hotter than any measured in the May 18th flows and nearly hot enough to melt aluminum. Fawn Lake bathymetry and Amazing Reverse Tree Blowdown

Fawn Lake lies nine miles north by northwest of Mount St. Helens, occupying a steep cirque with a 450- to 500-foot-high headwall. The headwall rises directly above the lake basin, on the side nearest Mount St. Helens. The mountain slopes nearby had been extensively logged by 1980, leaving forest only in the immediate lake basin.
Norm Dion, Leonard Nelson, Gil Bortleson, and Jim McConnell of the USGS Water Resources Division Tacoma office performed the initial bathymetric and water quality chemistry of Fawn Lake in September 1974, shortly before surveying Spirit Lake and other local lakes. Fawn Lake had an elevation of 3728 feet, a maximum depth of 84 feet, and a surface area of 20 acres. Water clarity in September 1974 was measured at 21 feet. 

The May 18, 1980 pyroclastic density current (lateral pyroclastic surge) dramatically affected Fawn Lake and its cirque. 
Susan Kieffer’s USGS research on the fluid dynamics of the pyroclastic surge hypothesized that the current dropped from supersonic to subsonic in steep locales such as the high cirque wall. As the lower portion of the current rapidly dropped in elevation, it reversed direction and caused spectacular tree blowdown patterns (see photographs and maps). The pyroclastic current also swirled into the basin from lower topography to the northwest and blew down trees in contrasting directions. Most trees immediately below the top of the cirque wall were snapped off well above their base.
The lateral surge also blew a huge amount of water out of the lake and down the outlet into Shultz Creek. Rescue 

helicopter pilots searching the devastation area on the eruption day noticed that the water surface level appeared to be 2-3 feet lower than normal, and that the outlet was dry. Researchers later found the outlet had been scoured by a downhill rush of water, and trees still rooted were left with 3-foot-deep wells created by fast-flowing water.

The magnificent picture at the end by Lyn Topinka shows Sandy Embrey and Jim Ebbert of the USGS WRD Tacoma sampling the water chemistry on Fawn Lake on a perfectly clear and chilly late October day in 1980, their last visit to the lake. Sadly, the Fawn Lake cirque was not included in the Mount St. Helens National Volcanic Monument, and the fantastic tree blow down patterns were erased by salvage logging.
I had fun making a large map on poster board with the tree blowdown directions, cirque elevation contours, and the bathymetry. There are even four different tree styles as direction indicators, tiny razor-cut stencils. When it starts raining in the fall, I will finish it up.
