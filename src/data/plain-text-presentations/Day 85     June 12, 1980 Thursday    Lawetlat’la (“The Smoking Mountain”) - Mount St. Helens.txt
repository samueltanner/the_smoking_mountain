Day 85     June 12, 1980  Thursday

USGS daily report
The third explosive eruption of Mount St. Helens occurred tonight. It was preceded by a buildup of harmonic tremor, which began at low levels about midday and increased amplitude throughout the afternoon. The University of Washington seismology center notified the Vancouver coordination center and the Washington Department of Emergency Services that a buildup in tremor similar to the one that preceded the May 25th eruption was occurring. A marked increase in the harmonic tremor was noted at 1905 hours, and reports of an eruption cloud rising to an altitude of at least 13,000 feet were received five minutes later. Tremor dropped off in strength immediately after the eruptive pulse and fluctuated at moderate amplitudes for more than two hours. This temporary lull was broken dramatically by a large increase in tremor amplitude at 2111 hours. Shortly thereafter, observers in a Forest Service airplane witnessed the very rapid climb of an eruption column to an altitude greater than 35,000 feet. About 400 families living in the latest Red Zone were warned to evacuate, but many reportedly declined to do so. A flash flood watch was put in effect for the Toutle, Cowlitz, Kalama, and Lewis Rivers. National Weather Service radar observers in Portland tracked the eruption plume to an altitude of 50,000 feet at 2118 hours. Forest Service airborne observers reported that the column was "several kilometers wide" at its base and was producing lightning. Cloud cover, however, prevented observers from seeing whether new mudslides or pyroclastic flows were occurring. The plume height as detected by radar fluctuated between about 15,000 and 35,000 feet above sea level until about midnight, when it diminished. Prevailing winds are carrying ejected ash to the south and southwest, and pumice particles as large as one-half inch across are falling 

"like hailstones" on Cougar, about 11 miles downwind from the volcano. Ashfalls began in Vancouver and Portland about 2250 hours and are continuing as of midnight. Recognition of the pre-eruption pattern of the harmonic  
tremor warned emergency-services officials of the impending eruption and allowed them to warn the public quickly. No casualties were reported.   [USGS PP 1249]

The Continuing Story of Mary & Ron Rinear and Blair Barner & The Buried A-Frame
Blair, Mary, Ron and friends began the huge task of digging the mudflow deposit out of the A-Frame during the summer of 1980. A big log and others smaller had bashed through the front of Blair’s A-Frame on May 18 and the entire inside was buried 4 feet deep with mudflow and debris. The following photographs of Blair's kitchen are crazy. Mary and Ron’s cabin sat just a few feet higher, and the mudflow and logs covered their front porch and foundation and then somehow stopped.
The A-Frame became more than a curiosity to passers-by, even among the many other buried houses and cabins along the branches of the Toutle River. The A-Frame had that clean little red triangle poking out of the huge flat of grey mud and it caught your eye. In September 1980, they started selling pamphlets and books about the eruption out in front of their cabins on the Spirit Lake Highway. The highway was blocked by state troopers just upstream of the cabins, and by the debris avalanche deposit a couple miles up from Maple Flat. Fascinated tourists bought absolutely everything they had. Pretty soon, they realized the A-Frame was most amazing when buried. So, they reburied it, put the battering-ram log out in front and started the Buried A-Frame educational center and souvenir shop. The place opened on Mother’s Day, May 1981. The aerial photograph of the shop and the A-Frame was taken in 1985, and Ron and Mary say it was constantly humming with volcano seekers.
Mary, Ron, and Blair owned the business together for nearly four decades. Would you believe they sold 30,000 Mount St. Helens ash pens?! The volcano was long the most popular tourist destination in Washington State, and most of those adventurers drove right past their cabins on the North Fork Toutle along the Spirit Lake Highway.

The giant Sasquatch in the photo with the Rinear’s children was made by a local artist and featured hair-like carpet that was pretty real-feeling. One summer day it caught fire and Ron describes it going up like a Roman Candle, scorching trees and a telephone pole near it. They think a visitor may have been playing around with a lighter. A concrete version of Bigfoot replaced the other and stands in its place to this day waiting for you to visit. Ron says he tried to convince Blair not to do the "Sasquatch" thing to draw business, but finally gave in. Everything at Mount St. Helens is Bigfoot this and that, no matter which side of the volcano you might be exploring.
Just a couple years ago, Ron decided he was not spending nearly enough time fishing. Mary and Ron sold the cabin and their share of the business, and now have more time for chasing salmon and steelhead and visiting the grandkids. On those days when Mary is substitute teaching at Evergreen Forest, Ron can probably be found on the Chehalis or maybe even the fabled Green River.
