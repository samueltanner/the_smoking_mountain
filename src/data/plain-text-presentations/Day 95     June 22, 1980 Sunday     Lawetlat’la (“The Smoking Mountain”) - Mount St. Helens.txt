Day 95     June 22, 1980  Sunday

USGS daily report
Mount St. Helens has spent a quiet weekend. No earthquake or harmonic tremor was recorded, but there were minor seismic jiggles, probably caused by avalanches. Persistent clouds of steam and fumes continue to rise above the crater to altitudes as high as 14,000 feet. Although the dome could be glimpsed within the crater, visibility was too poor to permit measurements of its growth. The scientific team continues to work hard to learn as much as possible about Mount St. Helens and, consequently, about this type of volcano in general. The main thrust of the work so far has been hazard evaluation; in this effort, many team members have been voluntarily working 16 to 18 hours a day. Geologist Rowley says that they really believe that they will learn enough from Mount St. Helens to help save many lives during future eruptions of other volcanoes. The USGS crew at Vancouver numbers about 20 to 25 at any given time, but many more can be recruited, if needed, from other USGS installations. At the pace now being maintained, "people get 'burned out,' " Rowley said, "and we bring others in." The official casualty count - 24 dead and 44 missing - presumably will change tomorrow. A geologist riding in a Forest Service helicopter today spotted another body, which will be recovered tomorrow, if possible.

Deadmans Lake bathymetry
After completing the bathymetry at Saint Helens Lake and packing up, Paul, Leonard and I planned to fly north to Deadmans Lake. Deadmans Lake sits just 2/10 mile beyond the Tree Sear Zone and the Goat Mountain massif, 12.7 miles northeast of Mount St. Helens. The lake lies at 4352 feet in elevation and its surface area is 32 acres. Tephra fall (rock and ash) were the only volcanic impacts. The lake was intended to serve as a control to determine whether other seemingly unaffected lakes close to the volcano had significant water chemistry and depth changes from tephra deposition. A 1950s Washington State lakes inventory (Wolcott) indicates a pre-eruption bathymetric map was made, but the map was never found. Perhaps Deadmans was only sounded; the estimated maximum depth was 60 feet. 
Paul elected to fly Leonard and I first, then return for the boat and remaining gear. Looking down as we circled for landing, Deadmans more familiar shades of blue and green were in such contrast to the blast zone. Long after we’d scrambled out and had finished a late lunch along the peaceful shoreline, the helicopter reappeared. As the craft neared, Leonard and I traded quizzical glances. The boat seemed to have a tree sticking from its side. Closer came the helicopter, and we saw that our boat had indeed collected flora in a most unorthodox manner. 
Paul unslung the boat and landed in a nearby grassy meadow, while we examined our battered boat and waited to hear the story. Paul crept over to the shore after several long minutes, looking as if he had seen a ghost. He had removed his door in order to keep an eye on the boat as he flew high over Mount Margaret’s ridgeline, then over the deep Green River valley and Goat Mountain to the north. Descending, he knew the boat was close to skimming the treetops when the impact jolt came. Paul had to instantly decide whether to drop the boat or continue flying. Luckily, the treetop pierced the side of the boat and snapped off, instead of entangling and 

possibly yanking the helicopter from the sky.
Looking back, I am not sure why Paul continued to fly a craft that was not performing well. Two USGS-contracted helicopters had crashed around the mountain in the year since the eruption, both caused by the very tricky winds on the rampart of the volcano (no one was seriously injured in either crash). Almost all of the pilots we flew with had flown in Vietnam, so perhaps exciting experiences like this might seem a bit benign compared to wartime flying.  
After completing the bathymetry, Paul took off with the first load and planned to fly down the low-elevation confines of the Green River. He had to circle the small meadow several times in order to gain enough altitude to pop over the trees. Leonard and I traded a few more glances, but we totally trusted Paul and had flown with him regularly over the past year. A half-hour later we were in the air, leaving the boat behind to be gathered another day. Our flight down the Green River was fascinating, as we wove through forests that were blown down, burned standing, or perfectly green. The mosaic of dead and living trees was intricate, and the margins between these zones were often only a few hundred feet or less. Hillsides of blowdown appeared to have been combed by a giant’s hand, and every gully was visible in the pattern. 
Paul touched down gently by our truck at Maple Flats near Blair Barner’s famous red “Buried A-Frame” and Mary and Ron Rinear’s cabin, and we bade him farewell moments later. Leonard and I loaded up, crawled onto the comfy bench seat, and headed off for dinner and home. All the way, I thought about how lucky I was to have such an event happen in my lifetime...and in my mountain backyard.

Back at the office the following day, I began studying the trisponder distance data and depth profiles from the fathometer chart roll for Saint Helens and Deadmans Lakes. Triangulating the data points on a large poster board was a pleasing task, especially as the bathymetric contours of Saint Helens Lake began to take shape. The bathymetries for Coldwater Lake and Spirit Lake were quite complex and challenging to draw, with so many underwater debris avalanche hummocks to interpret. Saint Helens Lake was decidedly simpler to map, but my brain kept imagining underwater islands. I told Leonard I was quite sure one existed in this new bathymetry as well.
Leonard looked at my rendering, and said “Hmmm”. Then he went to lunch.
A couple hours later, I told Leonard…”There’s no underwater island. It's a classic, deep, glacier-scoured bowl”. Leonard paused and said, “Yeah, I think you’re right”.
He could have immediately corrected me and told me I was a goofy 21-year-old kid, but instead he let me solve the problem. Pretty cool.
