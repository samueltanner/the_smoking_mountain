USGS daily report
Good weather again returned to the mountain, which continued to erupt intermittently. Airborne observers could see into the crater during a moderate eruption that took place from 0612 to 0658 hours. They saw large blocks of ice being hurled into the air along with ash and steam. They also noticed two new steam vents on the northwestern flank of the volcano, just below the crater rim, as well as ponded water on the floor of the crater. For only the second time since activity began 23 days ago, a magnitude 4.9 earthquake occurred today at 1551 hours. This quake and the similar one on March 28 have been the strongest of the volcanic earthquakes to date. The results of tiltmeter measurements on the volcano's northern and eastern flanks are, so far, inconclusive. Although the ground both swells and subsides over periods of minutes or hours, the net change seems to be insignificant. Signs now have been posted along Interstate Highway 5 notifying motorists of four safe points from which to view Mount St. Helens.   [USGS PP 1249]



On Friday, April 11, 1980, two young climbers and a cinematographer started up the volcano. 
Andy Sterne (age 21), Brian Witt (26) and Otto Sieber (41) climbed in gray parkas, pack covers, and gaiters to avoid detection. They were slowed on the upper flank by ash-laden water running quickly down the melting snow surface. An unplanned bivouac on April 12 required hacking out a snowy ledge at 8000 feet and sharing one sleeping bag. 40 earthquakes shook Mount St. Helens throughout the day, and even more kept them awake most of the night. Summiting the next morning, they were sighted by a Forest Service plane but were not intercepted. They came off the mountain on April 14. Their story appeared in The Tacoma News Tribune two days later on April 16.

Sieber began a second trip on Friday, May 23, 1980, less than a week after the cataclysmic May 18 eruption. Footage of that trip is included in Keeper of the Fire. Sieber and friends were hoping to reach Spirit Lake, but mired down many miles away near the Hanaford Lakes group and the new Coldwater Lake in even more fresh ash from the early Sunday, May 25 eruption. They had angered Skamania County Sheriff’s Deputy George Barker Saturday morning, when he flew in to cite them for trespassing. They were rescued on Monday night, hypothermic and ash-blinded. 
You’ll read about their exploits later in the Tacoma News Tribune clipping on Tuesday, May 27.

Historic photographs
The string of lakes behind Mount Margaret and Mount Whittier were beautiful and very popular. The lakes lie 9-10 miles NNE of Mount St. Helens. The Washington Department of Game estimated that 4100 visitor days were spent fishing pre-eruption on Mount St. Helens’ many lakes. 
Hiking from the east and climbing over Norway Pass and Bear Pass, Grizzly Lake is first in the string. Grizzly Lake is 8 acres in size, and a maximum depth of 55 feet was measured in the 1950s but no bathymetric map can be found. Grizzly Lake was also known as Mystery Lake.
Obscurity Lake (also known as Twin Lakes and Chain Lakes) comes next along the trail. Only a very fuzzy pre-eruption photograph, but the subalpine lake looks pretty. Obscurity was mapped by Lavier of the Washington State Department of Game on August 15, 1954.   
Boot Lake was a short climb above the pre-eruption Lakes Trail, but Boot basin is now closed to entry for research purposes and the Lakes trail still passes below the basin. Boot Lake is 16 acres in size. A maximum depth of 70 feet was surveyed in the 1950s, but no bathymetric chart exists.
