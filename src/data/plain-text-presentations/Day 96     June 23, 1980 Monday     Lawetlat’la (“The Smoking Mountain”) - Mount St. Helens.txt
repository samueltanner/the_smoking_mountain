Day 96     June 23, 1980  Monday

USGS daily report
This morning, a steam plume rose briefly-first to about 13,500 feet and then to only 11,000 feet before dissipating. Otherwise, neither eruptive nor seismic activity was recorded on the mountain. Most of the day, the mountain again was shrouded in clouds, and scientists could not see into the crater to observe the dome. 
USGS scientist Joseph Rosenbaum has reported on some of the details of the lateral blast - the great north-directed "wind" caused by the sudden release of gases that had been held within the magma. Blast damage was caused, he believes, not by a shock wave or by an ultrahigh-speed wind but by a hurricane-speed wind heavily laden with debris. Such a particle-laden wind that could "sandblast" everything in its path would explain the pattern of destruction, according to Rosenbaum. Trees, stumps, and even soil were removed from exposed ridges nearest the mountain; only bare rock was left. Farther from the volcano, trees were snapped off at the ground, and bark was cleanly removed from trunks. A normal wind would have had to reach speeds of 150 to 200 miles an hour to destroy the trees in that manner. If the density of the "wind cloud" were increased by suspended rock particles, however, a wind moving at lesser speeds could achieve the same results. Investigations in the devastated area give ample proof that the blast wind was carrying pulverized rock (stones up to 4 inches across carried at least 8 feet off the ground) as well as other debris picked up in its outward rush. 
The Forest Service is asking Congress for additional funds to pay for monitoring smoldering timber debris under the ash and blast deposits on and near Mount St. Helens. Several hundred fires caused by lightning and hot ejecta from the May 18 eruption still burn beneath the ash. The ash is acting as a fire retardant, but Forest 


Service officials expect that, when it dries and the wind blows it away, flames and sparks could spread fires to neighboring timber stands. 
Governor Ray announced today that tourism in Washington so far this year is only about half the amount expected, largely because of adverse publicity about ash dangers. Financial institutions across the country apparently are wary of investing in some Pacific Northwest bond issues because of disruptive effects and economic risks attributed to the ashfall from Mount St. Helens. A San Francisco bank official estimates that this perceived increase in financial risk could drive up interest rates for borrowers such as school districts and city or county governments in affected areas. 
The body sighted yesterday was recovered by a helicopter team today and identified by Lewis County officials. The official number of dead is now 25; 43 are still missing.   ​[USGS PP 1249]

June 23, 1980 Monday TNT news clipping

The distribution of the sound produced by the major eruption of Mt. St. Helens on 18 May 1980 has been determined from the reports of over 4000 observers. A zone of silence extended in all directions to a distance of 100 km from the mountain, beyond which the sound was heard to a distance of 930 km, with the greatest intensity between 250 and 300 km. The observed atmospheric temperature and wind velocity gradients indicate that total refraction of the sound occurred at an altitude of 50 km. This has been confirmed by calculations based on the times of arrival of the sound recorded by barographs. The original pressure pulse from the eruption appears to have been slow rising, but steepened to the form of a shock wave during transmission through the atmosphere. Most observers reported the arrival of several sound pulses but it is not known if these originated at the eruption, or were produced by stratifications in the atmosphere.

Zone of Silence - Distribution of Sound from Mount St. Helens' Eruption.    John M. Dewey University of Victoria, BC, Canada

Hanaford, Elk, and Forest Lakes are nine+ miles north by northwest of Mount St. Helens, about a mile to the northeast of Fawn Lake. Hanaford Lake was also known as Frances Lake, Forest Lake as Doris. The Hanaford group had also been extensively logged, save for the areas immediately next to the lakes. The USGS mapped Fawn Lake in 1974.
All five lakes are located in the Tree Down Zone, devastated by the lateral pyroclastic surge.

The string of lakes behind Mount Margaret and Mount Whittier were beautiful and very popular pre-eruption, and still are. The lakes lie 9-10 miles north by northeast of Mount St. Helens. 
Hiking from the east and climbing over Norway Pass and Bear Pass, Grizzly Lake is first in the string. Grizzly Lake is 8 acres in size, and a maximum depth of 55 feet was measured in the 1950s but no bathymetric map can be found. Grizzly Lake was also known as Mystery Lake. 
Obscurity Lake (also known as Twin Lakes and Chain Lakes) comes next along the trail. Only a very fuzzy pre-eruption photograph, but the subalpine lake looks pretty. Obscurity was mapped by Lavier of the Washington State Department of Game in August 1954.   
Boot Lake was a short jaunt above the pre-eruption Lakes Trail. Boot Basin is now closed to entry for research purposes and the Lakes trail still passes below the basin. Boot Lake is 16 acres in size and drains to Obscurity Lake. A maximum depth of 70 feet was surveyed in the 1950s, but no bathymetric chart exists. 
Grizzly, Obscurity, and Boot Lakes drain to Grizzly Creek and the Green River. All three lakes are located in the Tree Down Zone, devastated by the lateral pyroclastic surge.

Panhandle Lake is in the middle of the string and is still a wonderful place to overnight, right about where the horsemen in the old photograph slept. The picnic table did not survive the eruption. Panhandle was surveyed by Lavier of the Washington State Dept. of Game in August 1954, and supported both Rainbow and Eastern Brook trout pre-eruption. Panhandle Lake drains steeply north down to the Green River.  
Holmstedt Lake sits high underneath Mount Whittier’s north wall at 5100 feet, and drains to Panhandle Lake.
Shovel Lake is a gem. Easily my favorite in the string of mountain lakes, Shovel is a long ways from any trailhead. It was partially surveyed on that same day in August 1954. Shovel Lake (also known historically as African Lake) is 100+ feet in depth, drains to Panhandle Lake, and long ago regained its deep blue hue. Rainbow, Eastern Brook, Cutthroat, and Montana Black Spot trout once plied Shovel's depths.
All three lakes are located in the Tree Down Zone. 

The major impact of the eruption on the lakes in the Mount Margaret Backcountry was to the plankton and benthic communities. Likely due to snow and ice cover on May 18, aquatic insects, crayfish, freshwater clams, and plankton were reduced but not eliminated. In most of the MMBC lakes, plankton rebounded to pre-eruption levels by 1982. 
Fish survived in 70% of the lakes. Generally, Cutthroat trout died, but Brook trout survived.

Snow Lake, Heart Lake, and Bench Lake continue the string of lakes but are much smaller and lie on the west side of Pleasant Pass, the grassy divide between Shovel and Snow Lakes. Snow, Heart and Bench Lakes drain to Coldwater Creek, Coldwater Lake, and the North Fork Toutle River. 

Snow Lake lies 9+ miles NNE of the volcano. Also known as Burgoyne Lake after the homesteaders in upper Coldwater Creek valley. Snow Lake’s depth was measured at 20-30 feet, but no bathymetric chart was made. Snow Lake is located in the Tree Down Zone. 

Heart Lake sits in very protected pocket behind a cliff forming part of the Coldwater valley wall, almost nine miles NNE of Mount St. Helens. Heart Lake is in the Tree Sear Zone and most of the trees in the tiny basin were killed standing by the pyroclastic density current. Every single lake lying closer to the volcano and all but one up to 2+ miles further away than Heart Lake were less protected by nearby topography and are in the Tree Down Zone.   

Bench Lake sits just below Heart Lake and its protective cliff, 8+ miles NNE of the volcano. Tiny Bench Lake is also known as Balcony Lake because of its commanding view over Coldwater valley. Bench Lake is located in the Tree Down Zone.

Venus Lake and Lower Venus Lake

Venus Lake lies 10 miles NNE of the volcano. Primarily in the Tree Down Zone, the northern slope and end of Venus Lake are in the Tree Sear Zone. Drainage is to Lower Venus Lake and the Green River.
Lower Venus Lake is 10.4 miles NNE   In the Tree Down Zone, drains to Venus Creek and Green River

Norm, Len, Gil Bortleson, Jim Innes, and Jim McConnell performed water chemistry analysis at Venus on September 14, 1974 and a bathymetric survey on September 19. Venus Lake was exceptionally clear, with the Secchi disk visible at a depth of 66 feet. Maximum depth was 140 feet. Five months after the eruption, the water transparency was only one foot. By summer 1982, transparency improved to 24 feet. Venus showed the most improvement in clarity of any blast zone study lake.
Sandy, Norm, and the water quality crew spent a lot of time there post-eruption. 8 trips total, 5 in summer 1981.
The “before” photograph by Norm and the “after’” by Sandy are truly amazing.

Ryan Lake lies 12.2 miles NE of Mount St. Helens. At the far edge of Tree Down Zone, Ryan Lake drains to the headwaters of the Green River.

Norm Dion, of the USGS WRD Tacoma and a charter member of The Spirit Lake Yacht Club, volunteered to retell the story of The Infamous Ryan Lake trip

One objective of my Mount St. Helens Lakes project was to determine how the eruption had impacted the lakes along the trajectory of the ash plume. Ryan Lake was certainly one of those lakes, being just "down plume" of Spirit Lake. But we had never had an opportunity to go there and study it. However, we were in the last stage of the study, and today (date unknown) we were determined to get there and study it. (Never mind that we were about to break FAA rules by flying both personnel and a load on the same trip. Shhh!) Bad decision. Very bad decision!
It was at least noon when we started out, and it was quite windy on the ground. For whatever reason, we had a very new and very young pilot, who had never flown with a load before. Our usual Standard Operating Procedure was to A) sling the 12-foot aluminum runabout with 10HP motor, filled with pricey instruments, under the chopper using a homemade 4-point harness and take it to the lake in question. B) take those items to the target lake and drop them on the shore, then C) come back and get the personnel and take them to the same spot.
So off we go, after we show the young Lindbergh how to hitch up a load, of course. We're only a few feet in the air when we discover that the wind aloft is a tad stronger than on the ground. Did I just say "a tad"? But what the hell, it's only a 20-30 minute flight to Ryan; let's stick to it. One minute the boat is horizontal to our eyesight  

on the left, the next minute it's to our right.
Both Sandy and I are scared sh#$less and Lindbergh probably is as well but, to his credit, he won't show it. The chopper is being tossed back and forth like a ping-pong ball. We figure we have a last-chance out, however; we know which button drops the entire load; instantly. But what about all those pricey instruments, the samples we collected this morning, and the outboard? We decide to stick it out; after all, we'll be on the ground at Ryan in about ten minutes. 
Wrong! We finally get to the lake and the entire shore is choked with blow-downs and super thick brush and there's no way of getting boat, instruments, and personnel on that @#$%^&* lake. We have to turn around and go back, with our tails between our shaking legs. And the trip back is no better. I don't know about Sandy, but on arrival, I was ready to kneel and kiss the ground.

Such are the tales of those incidents as I remember them. You guys correct them and fill in as you see fit.  Norm

Bear Lake and Hoo Hoo Lake were located southeast of the mountain, on tributaries to Smith Creek and the Muddy River, respectively. Bear Lake was 5.9 miles ESE of the volcano and was essentially part of large marsh on Smith Creek shown on the USGS 1959 15-minute map. The marsh ran from Bear Lake north almost to the confluence with Ape Canyon. Hoo Hoo Lake (1600 WSL) was 6.6 miles from Mount St. Helens. 
Both lakes were small (<10 acres), and were rapidly becoming swamp. Both lakes were inundated by the massive lahars in the Smith Creek/Muddy River system on May 18, 1980 and no longer exist.

June Lake is located 3.6 miles SSE of the volcano. The water surface level was raised a couple feet by a small lahar which flowed into June Lake on its western side. The outlet appeared to have been unaffected by the lahar and the lake was not dammed. The lake drains to Swift Creek and Swift Reservoir (North Fork Lewis River).
