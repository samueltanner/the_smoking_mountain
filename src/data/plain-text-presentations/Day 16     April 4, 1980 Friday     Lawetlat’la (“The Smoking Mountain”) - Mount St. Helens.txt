The preceding “Photographs of the Day” were taken by Richard G. Bowen, a longtime scientist with the U.S. Geological Survey at Portland’s Water Resources Division office and with the Oregon Department of Geology. 
Mr. Bowen was also a pilot of fixed wing and glider planes. He took many of the very best photographs of the volcano, both these pre-eruption photos and shots of the cataclysmic eruption on May 18 and on the days immediately following. Photographs that rival the oblique aerials taken by Austin Post and Bob Krimmel of the USGS Glaciology office in Tacoma. 
I will share more of Mr. Bowen’s photographs later, especially photos showing the amazing run up (superelevation) of the eruption day lahar on the steep canyon walls of the South Fork Toutle River.  

Day 16     April 4, 1980  Friday
USGS daily report   27- and 32-minute episodes of harmonic tremor were recorded by seismographs today, as were five earthquakes stronger than magnitude 4.0 and many weaker quakes. Governor Ray called out the National Guard to share duties at roadblocks around Mount St. Helens. Only property owners and scientists are to be allowed inside the blockaded zone. In an effort to protect sightseers and to keep them out of the way, Washington State officials moved one roadblock on State Route 504 about 20 miles farther from the mountain and established a major public viewing area near Ridgefield, Wash., about 40 miles from the volcano. 
Loggers and truckers are urging county officials to let them resume work in the restricted zone, and they have offered to sign waivers releasing public officials from liability for any volcano-related casualties. Most scientists believe that the greatest hazard currently posed by the reawakened volcano is the possibility of flash floods or mudflows, especially those that might enter Swift Reservoir, and cause a massive overflow and flood surge into the downstream Lewis River channel and reservoirs. 
USGS hydrologist David Frank set up a time-lapse movie camera to take color pictures of the periodically erupting volcano. The camera, designed to expose one frame of film after each preset time interval, was installed on a logged-off ridge accessible only by logging roads, 8.4 miles northwest of the volcano's summit. The site on Maratta Creek overlooks the North Fork Toutle River near the mouth of Coldwater Creek and affords an unobstructed view of Mount St. Helens. It also is high enough to be safe from even the largest mudflow that might result if the ice and snow were melted by the volcano's heat. The site was selected a few days ago as a scientific observation post, and a tent was set up to shelter observers. Additional instruments, including another time-lapse camera, will be installed within the next few days. Scientists refer to the observation site as Coldwater, later called Coldwater I. The Spirit Lake/Mount St. Helens area was indeed a very busy place. The U.S. Forest Service was taxed to continue developing recreation facilities to meet the increasing use and demand. Major activities included camping, picnicking, boating, swimming, mountain climbing, snow play, fishing, hunting, horseback riding, hiking, backpacking, organization camping (YMCA, Girl and Boy Scouts, church) berry picking, spelunking, and driving for pleasure. The least represented outdoor group were probably the fishermen, since the clear, cold lake was not a productive fishery.

Camp Meehan  Portland YMCA  Known as the Spirit Lake Camp when opened in 1909, the name was later changed to Camp Meehan in honor of John Cooper Meehan (1888-1948) who was a long time supporter and employee of the Portland YMCA. It was first located near where Harry Truman's Mt. St. Helens Lodge would later be constructed. In the very early days, campers would take a boat from Portland to Kelso, a train to Castle Rock, and then trek on foot for two days to Spirit Lake. Cars and trucks would make the journey easier in time. One year after the end of World War II, the ever-popular camp relocated to its permanent lake spot at the northern  end of Spirit Lake’s East Arm. 
The October 1962 Columbus Day Storm heavily damaged Camp Meehan. Fallen trees damaged the property, causing the camp to be shuttered for ten years. After years of intermittent use, fundraising, and restoration by The Friends of Meehan, the camp finally reopened full-time in 1972 as a coed camp and continued until 1980. 
Longview YMCA   Camp Loowit  
In September 1929 the Longview YMCA Directors announced that a permanent site for a camp had been located at Spirit Lake, at Margaret (Lange) Creek on the East Arm. Al Raught, Harry Morgan, and the Weyerhaeuser Timber Company contributed enough lumber to start the original three buildings on June 14, 1930. Camp Loowit’s popularity increased after World War II, the hit a lull from the late 1940s through the 1950s. In the winter of 1958, there was a heavy snow fall and the following spring the lodge was leaning toward the lake with all the windows laying on the snow out front. A cable was hooked up at both ends and in the middle to pull the lodge back up. After a new Lodge was constructed in the early 1960s, and other improvements made, attendance climbed. A 1969 contest to rename the camp was won by a fourth grader, with her winning suggestion of Camp Loowit. 1000 campers attended during the summer of 1979.

• Duck Bay boat launch
• Timberline viewpoint
• Spirit Lake USFS Ranger Station and Visitor Center
• 2 parking lots, capacity 2,175 people
• 9 backcountry primitive camps (Saint Helens, Grizzly, Obscurity, Panhandle, O'Conner, Venus, Boot, Ghost and                Strawberry lakes).
• 7 organization sites with facilities for 370 people (Longview and Portland YMCAs, Epicopal church, Longview Ski Club Lodge, St. Helens Club cabin, Portland Girl Scouts, Portland Boy Scouts)
