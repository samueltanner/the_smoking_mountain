Day 93     June 20, 1980  Friday

USGS Daily report
No sizable steam or ash plumes were seen today. No seismic events were recorded, other than those that could be attributed to avalanches down the inner slopes of the crater. Cloudy weather prevented measurements of the dome. Last night, however, observers again saw the glowing red cracks in the lava dome, as they have for the past few nights. USGS hazards geologists said there is no way to know whether the dome will eventually build up to become the mountain's new top, as at least one volcanic dome became in the past, or whether this dome will be blasted out in a new series of eruptions. A University of Washington geologist believes that the growth of the dome is "probably the best thing that could happen" because, as long as the lava oozes out, the pressure in the magma chamber beneath Mount St. Helens is unlikely to build up to an explosive eruption. If the growth stops "for a period of weeks," he says, the danger of an explosion will increase. The Washington Department of Emergency Services announced that it has decided not to decrease the size of the Red Zone, as had been requested last Wednesday. 
Several streams that formerly flowed into the upper (eastern) part of the North Fork Toutle River were blocked near their mouths by the May 18th debris avalanche. These tributary streams include Coldwater, South Coldwater, South Fork Castle Creek, Studebaker Creek, and a few smaller streams. Ponds have formed behind the dams of avalanche debris, and water levels generally have been rising as the streams continue to flow into these ponds. Some of the ponds, which already contain large volumes of water, might continue to grow until they overtop or break through the debris dams. The consequent outflows could increase in volume as they incorporate the easily  

erodible debris, and a major mudflow coursing down the channel of the North Fork Toutle River and further damaging downstream areas might result. Geologists are assessing the stability of the debris dams, while hydrologists are calculating how much water is in the ponds and watching the rates of water-level rise to determine whether and when steps should be taken to drain the ponds artificially. Spirit Lake  Part VI
Oblique map of pre- and post-eruption Spirit Lake
A few years ago, I spent part of an evening sitting along the beautiful Shovel Lake shoreline sketching out how to create a large map/drawing to illustrate the big changes to Spirit Lake. I wanted the map to show both the pre- and post-eruption lakes and their water surface and lake bottom elevations; the switch in which arm had the maximum depth, East versus West; and the thickness of the debris avalanche deposit dumped into the lakebed.

Spirit Lake’s horseshoe configuration made this a bit difficult. I decided to do a profile for both the pre- and post-eruption lake, running a line through the progressively deepest parts of the lake on each bathymetric map, from Bear Creek’s inlet in the West Arm to Paradise Creek’s inlet in the East Arm. Almost like an oblique (“from an angle”) aerial photograph that allows you to see under the water as well. I made smaller profiles first in tiny increments to see if the map would work (and be accurate), then doubled the size two separate times and added color. The red lines on the following Spirit Lake photographs approximate the pre-eruption and post-eruption profile lines. 

I always liked that the sketch was labeled “Leonard’s Toe.” That was my nickname for the Eagle Point Peninsula that splits the two arms of Spirit Lake. I started calling it Leonard’s Toe while Leonard Nelson and I were waiting for our ride after bathymetric mapping in March 1981. We were hanging out on a flat on the peninsula 150 feet above the lake and I was taking photos in the waning light. The volcano was trembling a bit, and while we were enjoying the time to relax our Vancouver dispatch was anything but calm.
