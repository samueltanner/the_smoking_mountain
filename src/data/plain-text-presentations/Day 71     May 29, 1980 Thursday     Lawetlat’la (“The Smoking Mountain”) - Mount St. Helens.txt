USGS daily report
An Air Force SR-71 reconnaissance aircraft flew a mission today to take photographs showing topographic features of the altered mountain. Radar imagery obtained by the Oregon Army National Guard and reportedly showing a recently formed hump on the floor of the Mount St. Helens crater led to speculation that the volcano may be producing a lava dome. The presence of a lava dome, however, could not be checked by direct observation because steam and clouds permitted only occasional glimpses into the crater.
Emergency-services officials announced a critical need for hay and grain to feed animal survivors, both domestic and wild. Because of the danger of flooding on the Cowlitz River, the USGS issued an updated Hazards Watch to local officials. They pointed out that the capacity of the Cowlitz River channel has been reduced about 85 percent, from about 76,000 cubic feet per second down to about 10,000 cubic feet per second, a flow rate that usually is exceeded at least once every year.
Airborne search-and-rescue operations were ended at 1700 hours today. Altogether, nearly 18,000 hours have been spent in the extensive and dangerous search for victims and survivors. More than 100 people have been rescued since the May 18 eruption. The toll stands at 22 dead and 71 missing. Ground searches, however, continue.   [USGS PP 1249]

The Pyroclastic Density Current   (Lateral pyroclastic surge)  Part I
The Pyroclastic Density Current (PDC) was the most amazing aspect of Mount St. Helens’ May 18, 1980, eruption, and was the part that most surprised scientists. Mount St. Helens had erupted laterally at least twice in its history, but not to the magnitude of the May 18 blast. The volcano last erupted laterally about 1300 years ago.
When the one cubic kilometer landslide unroofed the volcano’s magma cryptodome and its geothermal system, the horizontal explosion tore through and above the second slide block of the debris avalanche at 0832.50 hours (about 30 seconds after the 5.1 magnitude earthquake; 20 seconds after the landslide began). †1 
Photographs indicate the ice, rock and searing gas mixture swept from the volcano’s vent and down the flank as a catastrophic, hot, expanding, opaque, and steep-fronted blast cloud, initially traveling at about 380 m.p.h. 
One eyewitness described the leading edge of the blast cloud as green tinged from the millions of trees snapped off or uprooted and pushed along. The average velocity of the pyroclastic density current was about 250 mph outward for the first four miles and it overtook the sliding Debris Avalanche about five miles out (0833.45) [+1:25]. Explosions at the 8500-foot level caused the south crater rim and third slide block to collapse and take the summit at 0833.50 [+1:30]. Another series of explosions formed a second pyroclastic density current that raced from the volcano. The surges were primarily undeflected by ridges and valleys until six miles out, and removed everything in their path and carried it away.

The second surge passed the first one approximately 7 miles from the vent at 0834.40 (+2:20). When the second surge came out from under the first, satellite sensors recorded it accelerating rapidly until 0834.56 (+2:36). Eyewitnesses on Mount Rainier (and others much closer) saw a dense, ground-hugging cloud 1000-1500 feet thick that followed the topography like a fluid, the front disappearing from view as it descended into valleys and reappearing as it ran up over divides. The climbers on Mount Rainier observed two distinct flows (pulses) more than one minute apart. †2
As the velocity dropped, the pyroclastic density currents became "channelized”. The surge followed stream valleys and deflected off of ridges and other topographical features, combing down every tree. The first PDC stalled and lifted at the 3-minute mark (0835.20), while the second PDC continued its path of destruction until the 6-minute mark (0838.20). This current was suddenly stalled and lifted by a strong north wind in the Green River valley and was partly blocked by the Mount Whittier massif, Minnie Peak, and other high peaks in the Mount Margaret backcountry, resulting in the very thin transition zone from blown down trees to seared trees in many locations along the northern to eastern margin of the blast zone.       
The Tree Removal Zone is labeled          on the Plate 1 map. Some trees were removed by the debris avalanche, especially where it overtopped South Coldwater Ridge. Others were removed by the wave pushed out of Spirit Lake as the landslide poured in. But the trees were primarily carried away by the traction load of the 

lateral pyroclastic surge, as the coarsest basal layers deposited were generally found in areas of complete tree removal. 
The Tree Down Zone is labeled         and extends outward up to 17 miles from Mount St. Helens, with nearly every tree blown down or snapped off. Notice the arrows on the map indicating that the tree blowdown was in many different directions, even towards the volcano in some places. †3

The Tree Sear Zone         at the very fringe of the devastation, was only several hundred feet wide in some places. The sear zone of standing scorched trees extended out as far as 18 miles to the northwest, and the transition from completely blown down trees to seared standing trees to healthy green trees         was very startling.

The surge devastated 230 square miles of forests, streams, mountains, and logged hillsides, from Ape Canyon Creek on the east flank to Sheep  Canyon Creek on the west, almost 180 degrees of azimuth east to west. The blast deposited 250 million cubic yards of gravel, wood, sand, and ash across that landscape, with accumulations from three feet in depth to a couple of centimeters.
