USGS daily report
Although the weather was very cloudy until late afternoon, the clouds finally broke up, and today was one of the best days for viewing the mountain since May 18th. Today's eruption pattern in the crater was like yesterday's; plumes of steam and other volcanic gases were rising above the crater rim. Observers saw one small ash cloud but concluded that the ash had avalanched down from the walls of the crater and was not new material. There is a lull in seismic activity; no earthquakes registered above magnitude 3 today. Although there were several smaller quakes, they were spread over a larger area than the quakes before May 18th had been. 
At the Elk Rock seismic station about ten miles northwest of the crater (one of several instrument stations that had been destroyed by the lateral blast), instruments were reinstalled today to help supply data for continuing studies of earthquake activity. A large area north of Mount St. Helens is dotted by phreatic explosion pits and craters from steam explosions. The steam is from underground water that is heated by hot avalanche and blast deposits. One vent near the southwestern end of Spirit Lake erupted steam 5,000 to 6,000 feet into the air early this evening, startling the pilot and passengers of a Forest Service airplane that happened to be flying over the area. A chemical analysis shows that the glassy ash blown out of the volcano on May 18th and since has the composition of dacite; this rock is formed from a type of magma that has a moderately high silica content and is generally quite viscous. By the end of the day, USGS hydrologists had installed new streamflow-measuring stations equipped for radio telemetry on the Cowlitz River at Castle Rock, on the Toutle River about six miles upstream from its mouth, and on the North Fork Toutle River farther upstream. 
Governor Dixie Lee Ray called out the National Guard to help clean up volcanic ash in Eastern Washington this 
weekend. A major manufacturer of dust masks donated 21,000 of them for use in ash-covered areas. Hay is 

being sought for animal herds in areas of thick ashfall in eastern Washington and for surviving animals near Mount St. Helens. State of Washington officials this evening released an estimate of more than $1 billion for most of the losses suffered in the State because of the May 18th eruption. Today is the beginning of the Memorial Day weekend, a time when Eastern Washington normally would receive hordes of vacationers from western parts of the State and from the Portland area. The Washington Department of Transportation, however, warned of windblown ash, continuing restrictions on road use, and limited public transportation in ashfall areas. These warnings, as well as the continuing closure of traditional recreation areas around Mount St. Helens, are expected to cause unusually heavy use of recreation areas in Western Washington, especially the Pacific Ocean beaches. The number of bodies recovered from the Mount St. Helens area rose to 17, but 71 people are still missing. The body of one man was found on the seat of his car; his camera was still held toward the volcano.   [USGS PP 1249]

Note: Over the coming weeks, I will continue posting photographs that were taken on that day 45 years ago.
Many other photographs, mainly from 1980-1981, will illustrate different aspects of the May 18 eruption along with maps, research links, artwork, and newspaper clippings. Hopefully, many of the photographs will be new to you. I am especially excited about the beautiful black & whites taken by Jan Fardell from 1980-83, and photos taken by my USGS colleagues from the Water Resources Division in Tacoma. Many slides were hiding in closets in Kodak Carousel trays and have never been seen until now.

Beginning May 26, we’ll spend three days discussing the Debris Avalanche. Succeeding days will cover the Pyroclastic Density Current (lateral pyroclastic surge), the Blast Pyroclastic Flows, the Plinian Column and ashfall, the Pumiceous Pyroclastic Flows, and the Lahars. Topics then turn to the new and affected lakes…Coldwater Lake, South Fork Castle Creek Lake, Spirit Lake, Saint Helens Lake, Fawn Lake, Venus Lake, and the other blast zone lakes. Plus, stories about superelevations of high-velocity lahars, the volcano’s missing glaciers, Crescent Ridge, and tales of friends and colleagues.
The subsequent summer and fall 1980 eruptions and spectacular pyroclastic flows out of the new amphitheater deserve their own slide shows too (May 25, June 12, July 22, August 7, and October 16-18).
