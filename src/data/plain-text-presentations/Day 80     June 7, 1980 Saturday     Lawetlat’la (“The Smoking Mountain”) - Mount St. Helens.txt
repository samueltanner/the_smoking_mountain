Day 80     June 7, 1980  Saturday

USGS daily report
Mount St. Helens continued its low eruptive activity today, sending steam plumes, but little ash, to an altitude of about 11,000 feet. No earthquakes were recorded, and harmonic tremor was as low as it has been since May 18. 
Weyerhaeuser, one of the largest owners of timberland in the Mount St. Helens area, reports that most of its downed timber seems to be salvageable and that the company expects to begin immediately restoring its equipment, facilities, and routes into the blowdown area. Loggers in ash-coated timber areas report that they must sharpen their saws about three times as often as they did before May 18.
Scientists in the field report the reappearance of small plants of the horsetail rush type, as well as small animals, in the devastated area. They also have seen fresh deer and cougar tracks in the ash. News reports remind the public that Mount St. Helens is not the only volcano now active. Mount Etna in Sicily is erupting and reportedly has killed nine people. Worldwide, probably 30 to 40 volcanoes are active now.   [USGS PP 1249]

The 17-mile-long Debris Avalanche deposit in the North Fork Toutle River valley incorporated water from the river and melting snow and ice (either blown from or temporarily remaining on the volcano’s slopes) to produce the massive mudflow in the valley beginning in the early afternoon of May 18. The mudflow began at about 1300 hours with water and debris running on top of the Debris Avalanche deposit. The building mudflow was first seen in the Maratta Creek area, about 8 miles from Mount St. Helens’ vent. The mudflow ran down both sides of the North Fork Toutle River valley, reaching Elk Rock (11 miles) in 25 minutes, then destroyed Weyerhaeuser’s large
Camp Baker logging facility at 1357 hours (about 20 miles from the volcano and two miles downstream of the distal end of the debris avalanche). The rapidly growing mudflow passed the mouth of the Green River (27 miles from the mountain) at 1530 and passed Mary and Ron Rinear’s cabin and Blair Barner’s red A-Frame next door a few minutes later (28.5 miles).
The mudflow’s average velocity above The Forks (confluence with South Fork Toutle at 38.4 miles) was 5 mph.
At 1810 hours, the Spirit Lake Highway 504 Coal Bank bridge near the town of Toutle was snapped off (39.2 miles), along with the Tower Road bridge located 49 miles from the volcano. The steaming mudflow reached the Toutle River’s confluence with the Cowlitz River at 2030 (92º F. temperature of mudflow and 55.6 miles

The mudflow eventually traveled 75.6 miles downstream from the volcano to the Cowlitz River’s confluence with the Columbia River. The Cowlitz River channel was reduced to less than 10% of its pre-eruption capacity by the volcanic sediment load. 
The Corps of Engineers began a race to dredge out 51 million cubic yards of sediment from the Cowlitz and Toutle Rivers, raise dikes and levees, and construct a debris retention dam on each branch of the Toutle, all before the winter rains came. The surveys of the two branches of the Toutle and the Cowlitz Rivers were great fun. Every two months from summer 1980 through 1981 as many as a dozen folks from the USGS Tacoma Water Resources Division would take over the Kelso Inn Motel and spend a couple days in the quickmud. With a jet boat and a small aluminum boat and an overland crew with rods and levels, we worked our way down the rivers running cross sections of bank elevations and channel profile. It was often pretty hilarious...abandoned hip waders in order to escape the mud, the jetboat coming to sudden stops on unseen ash bars, the aluminum boat sunk engine and all by a standing ash wave. All with the public often watching, shaking their heads and thinking about their tax dollars. There were millions of dredges and draglines working. Since I was the young goat, it was my job to jump out of the boat, trudge up through the deep mud in waders and heavy floatation jacket to the enormous drag lines and ask the operator to raise their line so our boats could pass. The drag lines were massive, needing to be delivered  disassembled on railroad cars and then reconstructed. Sometimes Leonard would get the jet sled up to 50 m.p.h. on the Cowlitz, faster than cars on the frontage roads. His passengers were mostly praying we did not find an ash bar. Long days on the rivers were always followed by loud dinners around a giant table at Bart's Restaurant in Longview.

​The following photograph shows Ron Lombard and Morey Miles surveying the scoured channel of the Main Fork Toutle River, surrounded by bayonet sticks filed sharp by the swift lahar. Ron was the main author of the two following reports. I wish I had taken more photographs of the surveys and the people involved, but the ash and mud was everywhere and rather tough on a camera.
​
Zoom in on the photograph of the crane and dredge bucket. Way in. When I first saw this photo several years ago, the caption read: Note USGS survey crew on the bank for scale. So I zoomed in and realized it was me and Maury Miles on the Cowlitz on November 4, 1981. I have no idea who the other guy is, or why he is wearing gray in a gray world. We wanted to be seen with all those big things swinging around and preferred USGS orange.   
Two favorite stories always come to mind about our days on the Toutle and Cowlitz Rivers.
The gang was surveying a cross-section on the broad, lower Cowlitz River. I was the chosen one to venture across to an ash bar for two shots on the channel edges. While waiting with a motionless surveying rod, I began sinking into the ooze. One hip wader submerged and I slowly retracted it. Once that leg was free, the other leg was now stuck. This went on for quite a while, while the gang shouted encouragement mixed with laughter. No one was coming to my rescue…they couldn’t, or they would suffer the same fate. Finally, I abandoned one hip wader and crawled back to the boat. It was pretty comical, but I was a bit concerned for a time.
The other one is a Cowlitz River story too. Our supervisor, Charley Swift, was along for the river survey and wanted a few shots on another one of those ashbars. I eagerly volunteered to impress my boss, swung off the boat, took two step in the river, found a hole and vanished.
