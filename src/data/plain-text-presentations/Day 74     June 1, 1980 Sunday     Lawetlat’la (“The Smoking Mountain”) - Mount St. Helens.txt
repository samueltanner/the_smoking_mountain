Day 74     June 1, 1980  Sunday
USGS daily report
The month has opened quietly. The volcano continues to vent steam, which rises in clouds to about 12,000 feet. Poor weather has prevented good aerial observation, but field crews, transported by helicopters, continued making ground observations and deploying monitoring instruments today. Measurements of the "new" Spirit Lake revealed that its depth is now about 100 feet or less, instead of its former depth of about 200 feet. The lake-water temperature was measured at 97°F (36°C) at the surface and 95ºF (35°C) on the bottom. 

Earthquakes were small and few, and harmonic tremor, which returned to the mountain a few days ago, decreased in amplitude by about two thirds early this morning. This decrease could mean that movement of magma under the volcano is subsiding, but no one knows. USGS geologists warn that, although the volcano appears to be relatively quiet now, it still remains dangerous to anyone nearby. If a lava dome were to build in the crater-an event that would be in character for this volcano-the dome building would likely be accompanied by more explosive eruptions, pyroclastic flows, and mudflows.

Today and tomorrow, we will investigate the Blast Pyroclastic Flow deposits 
of super-hot fine ash, which behaved differently than the Pyroclastic Density
Current, settling and ponding in low spots and valleys to depths of 60+ feet.   

The Blast Pyroclastic         deposits were part of the Pyroclastic Density Current which devastated 230 square miles of the mountain landscape, but the Blast Pyroclastic deposition occurred over a longer period (several minutes versus about two minutes). The blast pyroclastic consisted of dense portions of super-hot, super-fine ash suspended in the pyroclastic surge. The flows followed the topography and valleys, even flowing back toward the volcano in some places. Highly erosive, in places the blast pyroclastic stripped away the Pyroclastic Density Current deposits laid down only moments before, even stripping away pre-eruption bedrock. The bp deposits ponded to depths up to 60+ feet in valleys and low-elevation areas, after drifting down from steep slopes. Giant logjams were also transported downslope into valleys. Although the logs were emplaced as if from a flood of water, the deposits and logs were dry and hot, not wet, when examined two days after the May 18 eruption.

Large Blast Pyroclastic deposits were emplaced along many valleys, covering all of the South Fork Coldwater valley floor and a 3+ mile portion of Smith Creek to the east of the volcano. A blast pyroclastic flow covered the

majority of Studebaker Creek, which drains the northwest flank of the volcano. Spirit Lake’s Bear, Lange, and Paradise Creek valleys each contain a bp flow, and deposits were emplaced at many of the smaller lakes impacted by the blast (Saint Helens, Boot, Shovel and Grizzly Lakes) or created by the landslide (South Fork Castle Creek Lake).

The Blast Pyroclastic flow that deeply buried South Fork Coldwater Creek was one of the most amazing features of the May 18 eruption. The valley is becoming covered with willow and alder, but the completely flat blast pyroclastic deposit is still remarkable from the lower stretches of the trail up Coldwater Ridge. The present-day Highway 504 follows the downstream right bank of the valley, then snakes up South Coldwater Ridge all the way to the Johnston Observatory following a former logging road like the one visible on the left slope in the following photograph. The outlet tunnel from Spirit Lake flows under Harry's Ridge and dumps into South Fork Coldwater Creek. The tunnel opened in May 1985 and hydrologists were initially quite concerned about high discharges into a creek that had never seen such flows.
Mid-May 2023 brought record-breaking 90º+ temperatures to Western Washington. The super rapidly-melting snowpack saturated a portion of the May 18, 1980 debris avalanche deposit that had overtopped South Coldwater (Johnston) Ridge a short distance west of “The Spillover”. On Sunday evening, May 14, 2023, 300,000 cubic yards of saturated debris avalanche deposit slid. The Highway 504 bridge and road were destroyed at the hairpin turn over the Spirit Lake outlet tunnel exit, and the tunnel exit was nearly buried. Luckily, the highway is still closed until at least summer 2027 and hikers will find the Johnston Ridge parking lot empty after trekking up the fascinating Boundary Trail #1.
