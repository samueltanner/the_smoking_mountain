Day 81     June 8, 1980  Sunday

The fascinating first photograph shows unusual deposition of Mount St. Helens’ pyroclastic density current (lateral pyroclastic surge). The stratigraphic section was found approximately 10 miles northeast of the vent in the headwaters of the Green River. The stratigraphy shows the fine upper unit (Layer A2) of dark sand that was the base layer of the surge deposit at this distance from the volcano. The air fall unit (Layer A3) is light grey. The direction of flow of the surge deposit is to the left. I cannot explain how the tapering Layer A2 was deposited between the A3 layers, unless the deposition was influenced by topography and obstacles and the arrival times were slightly staggered. The stratigraphy is very different from the following photographs of other stratigraphic sections.

USGS daily report
Several nervous residents called the Mount St. Helens coordination center today to ask if the sharp lightning flashes visible over the volcano meant that it was erupting. It was not. The mountain remained quiet again today; only very low-level harmonic tremor was recorded at the University of Washington seismology center. Rain and clouds kept observation aircraft grounded, but an Oregon Army National Guard airplane flew over the mountain to obtain radar and thermal infrared images. Radar showed the base of a steam plume that rose lazily through the rain clouds to an altitude of about 12,000 feet. The thermal infrared images showed a general heating, since late May, of the eruptive vent that was active May 18th. The images also showed that heat was still being emitted by the pyroclastic flows. 
Scientists used the sporadic periods of clear weather this weekend to reestablish some of the survey lines on the southwestern side of the mountain for ground-deformation studies. The lines are to be measured as regularly as weather conditions permit. Observers are watching closely for the formation of a dome - a buildup of extruded lava that is too pasty to flow far from the extrusion vent. A dome could signal a rebuilding of the mountain, although not necessarily the end of danger. Following previous eruptions, Mount St. Helens has formed other domes; the now-vanished Goat Rocks was probably the latest such dome. Dome-building activity in the inner (May 25th) crater has been reported but not verified.   [USGS PP 1249]

Early debris dams on the Toutle River system

The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers began construction of small, preliminary sediment dams on the South and North Forks of the Toutle River in late summer/early fall 1980. The N-1 sediment dam on the North Fork was located just downstream of the distal end of the Debris Avalanche. The Corps moved mountains of volcanic mud in a race to create storage for the fast approaching winter rains and prevent catastrophic flooding of the Cowlitz River communities. 

Mount St. Helens erupted again on March 19, 1982, the first explosive eruption since October 1980. An ash cloud rose to a height of about 48,000 feet in the first five minutes. A second eruption began on March 20, lasting only a few minutes and sending an ash plume to about 18,000 feet. The eruptive activity produced an avalanche of pumice, ice, and rock debris. The avalanche and melting snow generated two mudflow events, which flowed north from the crater to Spirit Lake and the North Fork of the Toutle River. The 8-9 foot-high lahar breached the N-1 sediment retention dam 21 miles downstream of the crater.

Sediment Retention Structure on the North Fork Toutle River

The giant Sediment Retention Structure was constructed by Granite Construction Company of Watsonville, California, under contract to the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, Portland District. The SRS was built further downstream from the N-1 sediment dam on the North Fork Toutle River (near Kid Valley), and 13 miles upstream of the confluence of the North and South Forks of the Toutle. Construction of the $56.5 million structure began in December 1986, and was completed in December 1989. 
The structure began effectively trapping sediment prior to the winter of 1987-88. It was put to the test in January 1990 during heavy rainstorms that dumped nearly 13 inches of rain into the upper Toutle River Basin. The SRS was designed to trap sediment flowing from Mount St. Helens’ debris avalanche rather than to impound water. The structure performed its job well by impounding sediment behind the structure and provided flood control by temporarily storing storm runoff and releasing it through a six-tiered outlet system. The pool level behind the structure raised about 33 feet between January 5-9, 1990. The basin behind the structure was expected to fill by 2035, but instead quickly filled by 2012. The Corps of Engineers raised the spillway seven feet in 2013.
