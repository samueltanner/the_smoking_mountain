USGS daily report
Extensive cloud cover hid the mountain from view all day [the following news clippings are from the March 26 Tacoma News Tribune paper, but show March 25 photographs]. The University of Washington seismology center registered the 100th quake of magnitude 3.5 or larger in the Mount St. Helens area since March 20. Today, seven earthquakes of magnitude 4.0 or greater occurred, many less than the 25 recorded yesterday. Most of these quakes originated at relatively shallow depths of about 3 miles or less below the land surface. Seismic data processing and attempts to locate earthquakes' origins are now round-the-clock efforts.  
Despite the earthquakes and news reports, some residents do not believe that an eruption is possible. Said one, according to the Associated Press, "It's just . . . cooked up by the Federal forestry service for them environmentalists to delay a big development of the Spirit Lake recreation area. That's my opinion."

Fern Marlow, the mother of one of my sister’s best friends, kept an amazing scrapbook about the 1980 eruptions of Lawetlat’la. She carefully clipped out a newspaper clipping or two almost every day starting in March 1980 when Mount St. Helens first stirred. My sister rescued the book at a yard sale since Fern thought no one wanted it, and gave the treasure to me. Some of the clippings that I'll use are actually yesterday's news, since the TNT was an evening paper.  
It is a prize (and heavy). I refer to it regularly and this website has one or more TNT articles in almost every daily  presentation. The newspaper clippings fill in so many details of the Mount St. Helens story.

Historic photographs
Robert and Minnie Lange were the first white homesteaders near the shores of Spirit Lake, arriving in 1879 and building a trading post one mile downstream of Spirit Lake. Andy Oleson discovered gold in 1891 on Paradise Creek, which flows to the head of the East Arm of Spirit Lake. In 1892, a 156 square mile mining district was designated in the Spirit Lake/Green River area. Mr. Ludluck, a developer, built a mining camp near Ryan Lake in 1895. His company owned the Minnie Lee, Independence, and Lost Hope mines on the Green River. Two of the original cabins were still standing in May 1980, but were flattened by the lateral blast. Hundreds of claims were staked around 1900, and to facilitate gold mining Cowlitz County paid Mr. Lange to build a puncheon road in 1901-02. The road went up the South Fork Toutle River valley, crossed over Green Mountain to the North Fork Toutle and on to Spirit Lake. The incredibly rough road was rebuilt in 1929 by the county. 

The Lange Mine was situated on Lange Creek on the slopes below Mount Margaret at about 4000 feet in elevation. Mr. Lange was prospecting for gold and silver, but copper ore was the mineral chiefly extracted. After the mine played out, Lange turned his efforts to his sawmill.  
Dr. Henry Coe worked the Coe (Sweden) Mine on Paradise Creek, one mile east of the Lange Mine. The Sweden mine had a 2200-foot tunnel. The head of the east arm was known as Coe’s Cove, later the site of the Portland YMCA’s Camp Loowit. Coe’s Dam at the outlet of Spirit Lake was built around 1900 to regulate the water surface level, and the dam raised the water level of Spirit Lake about five feet. In 1905, 14 tons of copper ore from the Sweden mine were transported by barge across Spirit Lake and then by wagon 38 miles to Castle Rock. The ore was hauled to the Tacoma smelter and used to cast a bronze statue of Sacajawea for the Lewis and Clark 

Exposition held in Portland, which commemorated the 100th anniversary of their expedition. This was the only ore that was ever removed from the St. Helens mining district and smeltered. 
Profitable extraction of minerals was soon deemed unlikely, even after 400 mining claims, thousands of prospect pits, and 75 tunnels dug with more than 11,000 feet of underground workings. World War I essentially ended activity in the mining district. By 1929 most of the mines had been abandoned, although exploratory work continued sporadically until the 1980 eruption.

Ole Peterson homesteaded on the southeast side of the volcano, near the confluence of Pine Creek, the Muddy River, and the Lewis River. He discovered many of the lava tubes in the area that are well known today. 
Scores of cabins and shacks were built around Mount St. Helens in the early 1900s and most were abandoned a couple decades later. These structures were mainly clustered on the North Fork Toutle River near the Maratta Creek confluence, on Spirit Lake’s shoreline, near Norway Pass, and in the upper Green River valley.
