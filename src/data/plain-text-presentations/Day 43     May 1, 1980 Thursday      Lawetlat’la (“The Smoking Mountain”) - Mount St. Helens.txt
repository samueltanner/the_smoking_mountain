Day 43     May 1, 1980  Thursday

USGS daily report  
As the month opens, the mountain is steaming continuously but otherwise is not erupting. USGS observers report that a small lake has formed again in the volcano's crater and has covered one of the steam vents and that gas bubbles up through the water. The most active steam vents, however, are above the lake surface on the southern side of the crater. The swelling on the northern flank continues. One point on the bulge, according to measurements, moved about two feet outward in less than 12 hours. 26 earthquakes stronger than magnitude 3, and five of magnitude 4 or greater were recorded today. 

A seismic station was installed today at Dogs Head to help detect earthquakes that might trigger a major avalanche and to improve the accuracy of earthquake location. Near the 7,500-foot level, it is the highest instrument station on the mountain. A new observation station was also established today on a ridgetop east of the Coldwater I site and 2.7 miles closer to Mount St. Helens. The new site is between the valleys of the North Fork Toutle River and South Coldwater Creek, 5.7 miles north-northwest of the summit. It is at an altitude of about 4,100 feet, about 1,400 feet above the North Fork valley floor. Automatic cameras, other monitoring equipment, and radios for communication have been moved there from Coldwater I. Also, a travel trailer has been brought in to shelter volcano watchers, who now are at the site 24 hours a day. The new station is called Coldwater II.   [USGS PP 1249]

Historic Photographs
Our slow-moving airplane is leaving the Shoestring Glacier and cruising around the southeastern flank of Mount St. Helens, going clockwise. I'm learning some geology on the fly, especially about the many dacite domes around the volcano, like East Dome. East Dome commands Mount St. Helens’ eastern flank from the Plains of Abraham and Ape Canyon, and was likely extruded during the Sugar Bowl eruptive period.

The Worm Flows of andesite lava are only about 450 years old. The eruptive phases at that time and during 1800-1850 followed a pattern of large pumice eruptions, growth of the summit dacite dome, and then flank eruptions of andesitic lava and pyroclastic flows. 

My hiking pal Michele Williams and I crossed The Mitten lava flow (last photos) seven years ago on a weirdly warm morning with the temperature headed for the 90s. The crossing was arduous and tricky, as the lava boulders were prone to a bit of moving and rolling. Luckily, we did not have to cross in the fog and try to route find as well.
