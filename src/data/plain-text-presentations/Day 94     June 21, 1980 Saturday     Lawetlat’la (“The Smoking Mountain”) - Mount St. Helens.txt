USGS daily report
The volcano is very quiet. When the dome was measured last on Thursday, it was growing at a rate of about 6 to 10 feet a day, and so the crater area still must be considered unstable and dangerous. Clouds and plumes of steam that rose to an altitude of about 11,000 feet obscured the view into the crater. Seismometers on the mountain's flanks were jiggled today but only by avalanches of rock crashing down the inner wall of the crater. About 180 National Guardsmen used shovels and powered street cleaning equipment today in Portland to assist in cleaning up ash from the June 12 eruption. About 235 of an estimated 350 miles of main streets already had been cleaned before the Guardsmen started. Air quality generally improved throughout the city. The Oregon Department of Environmental Quality suspended an air pollution warning for the Portland metropolitan area, but it kept in effect the warning for Tillamook County on the coast. 
The mayor of Ritzville, a farming community of less than 2,000 people that received more than 3 inches of ash on May 18, said that conditions are far from normal now, more than a month after the ashfall. The ash is still very troublesome, despite valiant cleanup efforts by local residents and National Guard troops. The town is surrounded by flat to gently rolling farmland, and, when the frequent winds blow, the ash cannot be prevented from "redistributing itself." The mayor added that, although about 65 percent of the ash in town has been cleaned up, it is the other 35 percent that is causing much of the problem.   [USGS PP 1249]

Saint Helens Lake occupies a beautiful, classic, glacier-scoured cirque and bowl seven miles directly north of Mount St. Helens. The cirque was carved during a glacial advance from 22,000 to 11,000 years ago, when a glacier reached 19 miles down the North Fork Toutle River valley. The cliffs of Coldwater Peak tower over the lake and drop almost directly into the water. The lake basin was luxuriantly covered in old growth forest prior to the eruption and was a favored destination of Scouts and backpackers. The first photograph of Saint Helens Lake from Coldwater Peak in 1978 is one of my favorites, another great pre-eruption shot by Jim Nieland (USFS). The “after” photo was taken on a late September hike 40 years later. The next “before” photograph of the boys on the raft was taken by my former neighbor, Vermont Koistinen, on a 1965 hike with his sons. Monte grew up in Longview and frequently took his family on hikes and climbs around Mount St. Helens. I regret not asking to make copies of other wonderful slides Monte took on those trips. The 1950s and 60s automobiles and clothing are fantastic, along with their dress for summiting the mountain.
No pre-eruption water quality analysis or bathymetric survey was ever performed, but the lake had been sounded and was known to be in excess of 250 feet deep. At 4567 feet in elevation, the lake had a surface area of 79 acres and had Eastern Brook and Rainbow trout. The inlet had a hiker’s shelter, and the Bear Creek outlet dropped through an amazing bedrock lip 1350 feet down to the West Arm (Bear Cove) of Spirit Lake. 

On the morning of May 18, 1980, the surface of Saint Helens Lake was covered with ice and the basin was deeply buried in snow. The lake sits too high above the North Fork Toutle River valley to have received part of the debris avalanche flow, but the pyroclastic density current deposited rock, wood, and ash to a depth of about three feet throughout the basin. Every tree was blown down or snapped off. The ancient forest on the western and  

northwestern slope above the lake was completely removed and carried away by the pyroclastic surge. A dense log raft was left on Saint Helens Lake, with trees even stacked on top of each other and partly floating. The logs covered thirty to forty percent of the surface. Some small trees and vegetation that were deeply covered by snow survived the surge and poked through the rock and ash deposit with amazing speed.
A small blast pyroclastic flow was emplaced at the inlet on the northeastern shore and 1000 feet up the creek bed. Blast pyroclastic flows were created by especially dense clouds of suspended ash drifting and ponding in low areas after the gravel- and wood-laden lateral pyroclastic density current passed by. 
The post-eruption topographic map has the water surface level eight feet higher than the pre-eruption map 
(4575 versus 4567 feet in elevation). This is due to mapping improvements, not from the tephra dumped into the lake by the eruption. Pre- and post-eruption photographs of rock outcroppings along the shoreline show that the water surface level and the outlet’s bedrock lip were essentially unchanged by the eruption.

In late October 1980, Sandy Embrey and Jim Ebbert flew to Saint Helens Lake for the second water chemistry analysis. Sandy and Norm Dion had made the first visit two months earlier on August 28. 
When Sandy and Jim were attempting to launch the aluminum boat, Sandy didn’t realize a shoreline log had trapped a lot of silt and ash on the shore side. The water on other side of the log was several feet deep. As soon as Sandy tried to wade out a bit from the log her leg found nothingness and down she went, in hip waders in late October with a fresh snow dusting all around at 4500 feet!
Sandy will be forever famous for taking the first swim in post-eruption Saint Helens Lake.

Saint Helens Lake bathymetric survey
On the beautiful morning of July 14, 1981, Leonard Nelson and I were eager to fly to Saint Helens Lake and perform the first bathymetric survey of the deep and beautiful lake. With the boat slung on a 60-foot cable, I accompanied our favorite pilot, Paul, and a pile of instruments on the first trip. As we began our long ascent from the river valley floor, Paul mentioned that his helicopter seemed shy of its normal power and lift. 
I figured the boat and heavy load were the problem and went back to gazing at the devastation zone from our bubble.
Saint Helens Lake hid until the final moment, then instantly came into full view when the chopper rose over the bedrock rim that held the lake in its bowl. Paul set the boat down gently at the inlet and landed nearby so I could jump out and unload the instruments. Quickly unencumbered, the noisy craft rose again and sped off out of sight. Figuring a photo session was the first order of business, I clambered up the mountainside over and under shattered logs. When the volcano’s crater and new lava dome were visible through a ridgeline of limbless, snapped-off trees, I began clicking countless images and hoped my camera skills were up to snuff.
The lake’s color was coffee-with-cream, and each direction was a scene of remarkable destruction. Still, everything was spectacular.

A thick blanket of valley clouds cloaked the Spirit Lake basin below the perfect outlet rim, and I imagined myself peering over the edge of the earth. Brilliant green leaves of a false hellebore poked up through a thick layer of ash, a show of green in a world of brown and grey. The new growth contrasted sharply with the many splintered stumps of once-tall trees. A few slow mosquitoes buzzed about and made known their continued existence. The two-way radio crackled annoyingly with the constant chatter of other researchers awaiting fast rides to various places. As I sat awaiting the arrival of Paul and Leonard, Vancouver’s dispatcher finally radioed to inform me our helicopter was still experiencing lift problems. She added that our remaining equipment would have to be ferried in small loads to Saint Helens Lake, and that the process would take time. 
Remaining in that spot for the next hour and a half admiring the view, I turned the radio off a couple times but sadly never for long. When I did, not a single sound was carried on the slight breeze. The inlet was mostly dry, and the outlet’s flow slipped away secretly. I had spent lots of time working around the volcano in the year since the eruption, but at that moment I was finally and fully aware of all that had happened.  

Saint Helens Lake's bedrock rim and outlet. Note aluminum boat at inlet, bottom of photo. July 14, 1981  USGS K. Tanner

The silence was suddenly broken as the helicopter crested the rim with another load, sped off, and then returned once again with Leonard. We got to work quickly, set one trisponder remote near the inlet and then motored across the brown water to place the second. With a known distance between the remotes, we turned to mapping. Barely away from shore, the fathometer quickly began recording deep depths as we ran easy cross-sections across the roundish lake. The floating logs were nicely pushed up against the southern shore, and the lake’s  cooperative shape meant the trisponder remotes would not need to be moved and we could finish our work in one setup. Nearing the lake’s center, the fathometer marks went right down off the roll chart at 250 feet. Leonard, the old pro, quickly pulled out a second, smaller fathometer, which soon chalked up a maximum depth of 285 feet. Leonard and I were duly impressed.
No pre-eruption water quality/chemical analysis of Saint Helens Lake had been performed either, so changes to the water chemistry by the blast were unknown. The lake’s transparency was measured by secchi-disk in August 1980, with the 20-centimeter Secchi disk target visible only to a depth of six inches. By the end of the limnological study in 1982, Saint Helens Lake had the least improvement of any of Mount St. Helens’ impacted lakes with a transparency measurement of only 2.5 feet. Sandy Embrey and Norm Dion of the USGS predicted it to be the slowest of the impacted lakes to recover, primarily due to its great depth and longest water-exchange rate (5.4 years). They believed Saint Helens Lake might take decades to recover.

Reverse Tree Blowdown at Saint Helens Lake
An amazing phenomenon of the May 18 pyroclastic density current occurred at Saint Helens Lake. When the lateral current topped the high cirque wall closest to the volcano and immediately above the lake, part of the density current reversed direction while the rest continued to race northward. Trees high on the wall or atop the ridge were snapped off at least halfway up their trunk, while almost every tree on the cirque slope below were blown down towards Mount St. Helens. The ancient forest that once cloaked the steep lakeshore and gentle saddle under Coldwater Peak was in direct line of sight of the volcano, and was completely removed. 
This flow reversal happened in several locales in the blast zone, normally directly beneath the northern side of ridgelines, but the displays at Saint Helens Lake and Fawn Lake were especially spectacular. 

A fun future project would be mapping the orientation of the reverse blowdown trees at Saint Helens Lake. 
Truly mapping the trees in the field would have been exciting and challenging many years ago, but the vigorous willow and alder would make it nearly impossible today. Also, the logs continue to rot, and many have nearly disappeared or have been moved by snowpack. Luckily, high-resolution vertical aerial photographs from 1980 still exist in state and federal archives and would work nicely.
