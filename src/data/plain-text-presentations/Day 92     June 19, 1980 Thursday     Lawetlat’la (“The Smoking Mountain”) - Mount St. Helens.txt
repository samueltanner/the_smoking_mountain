Spirit Lake Fills and Fills
The beautiful previous photograph by Lyn Topinka (USGS) is one of the most widely circulated shots of Mount St. Helens and Spirit Lake. Taken from near the Eagle Point Peninsula two years and one day after the giant eruption, the lake surface had risen from 3404 feet in elevation to 3420 feet on May 5, 1981 (near completion of our bathymetric mapping) and was now dangerously full at 3453 feet on May 19, 1982. 

Spirit Lake’s outlet to the North Fork Toutle River was instantly dammed by the first slide block of the massive debris avalanche. The Spirit Lake blockage ranged from 120-440 feet in thickness. With the ever-deepening water surface at 3458 feet in elevation on August 1, 1982 and winter storms quickly approaching, there was great concern of a catastrophic breach. Two critical low points in the crest of the Spirit Lake blockage were surveyed in June 1982. Critical Point North (CPN) lay at 3532 feet in elevation and Critical Point South (CPS) at 3536 feet, meaning Spirit Lake was only 74 feet from the crest of the debris avalanche. However, steep-walled channels were found and observed in spring 1982. The channels were up to 65 feet deep and were created by water piping through the easily eroded pumiceous pyroclastic flow layers which sat on top of the debris avalanche. Although the channels were extending headward to the crest of the blockage at CPN and CPS, the crest had not yet been affected. At the base of the pyroclastic flow deposits at Critical Point South (CPS), the top of the debris avalanche was at 3506 feet, meaning time was truly in short supply.   [USGS Bulletin 1789]

Charley Swift and Dave Kresch of the USGS Water Resources Division Tacoma began hypothetical dam-break models and found Spirit Lake to be unsafe over time at 3460 feet due to potential piping, but safe at 3440 feet. Swift and Kresch found that if Spirit Lake breached at 3475 feet in elevation and 314,000 acre feet volume, it would have created a Magnitude 10 mudflow compared to the May 18, 1980 eruption day mudflow. The peak discharge would be 530,000 cubic feet per second (cfs), and after entraining sediment deposited by the debris avalanche, the peak discharge would be 2.65 million cfs. This mudflow would have inundated Castle Rock to a depth of 60 feet, and Kelso and Longview to depths of 30 and 40 feet, respectively. The potential mudflow from the dam-break model would reach the Toutle River confluence with the Cowlitz River at Interstate 5 (river mile 52.4) in approximately 4-5 hours.

The National Weather Service (NWS), which had the mandated responsibility for giving flood warnings, and the USGS Vancouver office installed a hazard-warning system consisting of three reporting stations on Spirit Lake and two on the channels below the lake. The agencies devised a warning system using tripwires, seismic signals (from a potential dam collapse), and river and lake stage heights at gages. The data was transmitted in real time by GOES satellite. The system could send alarms to USGS Tacoma hydrologists carrying beepers (Phil Carpenter, Chuck Swift, Ed Prych, and Dave Kresch). In the event of a validated alarm, the hydrologist on duty would alert the Cowlitz County Sheriff and the NWS, and a flood warning would be sent out.   [USGS Tom Pierson]

U.S. Army Corp of Engineers Begin Pumping Spirit Lake 
In order to maintain Spirit Lake’s water surface level at or below its “critical” elevation of 3462 until a more permanent solution could be found, the Army Corps of Engineers began building a pumping barge and excavating a trench for an outlet pipe in late summer 1982. A temporary road was built from Windy Ridge to Spirit Lake’s western shore near the toe of Harry’s Ridge. Pumping began November 5, 1982, with 16 diesel pumps and 4 in reserve. Discharge was through 3650 feet of 60-inch pipe. The Corps of Engineers began pumping water from Spirit Lake at a rate of approximately 170 cubic feet per second in November 1982, and a month later increased pumpage to 180 cubic feet per second. The low overflow point (3532’) in the Spirit Lake blockage was raised and stabilized at 3540’ when the discharge pipe was installed and buried. The highest WSL for post-eruption Spirit Lake was recorded at 3465.3 feet on March 14, 1983 when the pumps were undergoing maintenance and the crews were gone for two weeks due to increased volcanic activity. Pumping was also suspended during the dry months for maintenance from July 31, 1983 until September 22, 1983.

Six Alternatives for a Permanent Outlet of Spirit Lake
The Army Corps of Engineers, the US Geological Survey and the US Forest Service (along with state and local agencies) considered six alternatives in an USACE environmental impact statement (EIS) for a permanent outlet of Spirit Lake. 

The six alternatives were:
1) an open channel across the debris avalanche blockage to the North Fork Toutle River
2) a gravity-fed conduit buried in a trench through the blockage to the NFTR
3) a permanent pumping facility with a buried pipe across the blockage to the NFTR
4) a tunnel excavated through bedrock under toe of Harry’s Ridge to the NFTR
5) a tunnel through bedrock under Harry’s Ridge to South Fork Coldwater Creek
6) a tunnel through bedrock to Smith Creek and the Lewis River drainage basin

U.S. Army Corp of Engineers Bore Spirit Lake Outlet Tunnel under Harry’s Ridge
Alternative #5 was ultimately chosen, largely because it presented an almost invisible outlet to the millions of future visitors to the Volcanic Monument, and it seemed the most maintenance free. Both the inlet and outlet of the tunnel are quite hidden. There was ample concern, however, about introducing much higher flows into South Fork Coldwater than the valley had ever held. The South Fork Coldwater Creek valley was also of huge scientific importance, as an amazing blast pyroclastic flow had covered the valley floor with a flat-topped deposit and the flow’s dynamic was still being studied. Potential scouring by high water discharge from Spirit Lake into the valley would likely erase the deposits before those studies could be completed.

The outlet tunnel was bored under Harry’s Ridge from South Fork Coldwater Creek to Spirit Lake’s West Arm and opened on May 6, 1985. The tunnel is 8,463 feet in length at a 1.063% grade and 11 feet in diameter. 55% of the new tunnel was through “good” rock and required no support. Steel fiber-reinforced shotcrete was required in fractured rock sections. The 300-foot-long JKL shear zone posed difficulties during tunnel construction. and a rib set-shotcrete system was used for support. 
The tunnel intake is a four-foot-thick concrete headwall with a single conduit and a single 4’ x 4’ cast iron slide gate on the downstream face of the headwall. The bottom of the gate is at 3,436 feet, and flow through the intake falls down a vertical shaft. 
Spirit Lake began to be drawn down from a 3462’ water surface level to a 3440’ WSL (275,000 acre feet volume to 212,000 acre feet). The design allowed the inlet to be lowered to an elevation of 3,400 feet if warranted by future geologic events.

In 1995 and 1996, a fault heaved and cracked the concrete-lined tunnel floor. Large sections of concrete pulled away from the tunnel wall and the supportive ribs buckled. Major repairs required extended closures of the tunnel gate and outlet flow, which allowed the lake to rise all the way to the maximum safe operating level, topping out at 3,460.13 feet on February 5, 1997. The tunnel needed even greater repair in the same section in 2015/2016. The tunnel floor had been substantially raised (3-5 feet), causing the tunnel to be unable to carry its design discharge of 550 cubic feet per second (cfs). The current maximum allowable flow is 400 cfs. The constriction could also have caused the flow to become turbulent and “bottle”, stopping the water and potentially destroying the tunnel. Repairs were made, but the geologic fault was still on the move under Harry’s Ridge. 

The following photograph shows The Mole, the 112-ton Robbins tunnel-boring machine used to drill the tunnel. The Mole is 55 feet long, with an additional 400 feet of rail-mounted trailing gear. The tunnel, constructed under contract with the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers and located in the Mount St. Helens volcanic national monument, is under jurisdiction of the U.S. Forest Service. The Corps oversees contracts for annual inspection and maintenance of the outlet tunnel at the request of the U.S. Forest Service.   [USACE Portland]

Spirit Lake Intake Gate Replacement and Geotechnical Drilling Project
A Notice of Proposed Action was issued by the US Forest Service in December 2019 to replace the intake gate to the Spirit Lake outflow tunnel. The gate’s infrastructure was aging and lacked redundant safety features. 
The Notice also proposed Geotechnical subsurface drilling on the debris avalanche blockage. The NEPA (National Environmental Policy Act) process was completed in 2020. A public comment period ended with a majority of the responses against exploratory drilling…the respondents didn’t want a road reestablished for equipment and drill rigs and were adamantly against the inevitable disruption of many long-term research plots. Despite the objections, the USFS ruled that the tunnel repair and inlet gate replacement is a necessary maintenance and safety concern that the Forest Service is responsible for. 
In March 2021, researchers and conservation organizations represented by the Western Environmental Law Center challenged the plan to build the temporary access road through the Pumice Plain. The lawsuit alleged the USFS had not done the proper environmental assessments, or properly weighted the importance of the research or the damage the road would cause. 
On Dec. 22, 2021 District Court Judge Robert Bryan ruled in favor of the Forest Service, finding the agency did not act “arbitrarily, capriciously, or contrary to law.”

Replacement of the intake gate requires reconstruction of the road across the Pumice Plain that was used in 1985 to construct the existing tunnel and inlet. The current single steel gate would be replaced with a double redundant gate system with two flow paths, each through two separate gates. A loading facility would be 

constructed at the lakeshore for barging equipment to the existing tunnel inlet. A coffer dam would be built to dewater the existing intake gate structure during construction of the new gate. Geotechnical subsurface drilling is scheduled to begin 2025 and be completed by 2027. Information obtained from the drilling will help determine the feasibility of potential outflow alternatives through the debris avalanche blockage for long-term management of Spirit Lake water levels.

Spirit Lake Outflow Safety Improvement Project
The Outflow Project is charged with reducing the risk of failure of the debris avalanche blockage which dammed Spirit Lake’s outlet on May 18, 1980. The 1985 tunnel will require extensive future repairs to remain serviceable, and currently is operating at a reduced discharge rate. Tunnel failure could result in the only lake outflow being blocked for an extended time period, and with rising lake waters potentially causing a catastrophic breach through the debris avalanche deposit. The project is being designed to 1) accommodate water surface level rise during flood events without exceeding the 3460-foot safe level; 2) improve access reliability to outflow infrastructure. Access can be impacted by road length and rough terrain, barging equipment across the lake, the floating log mat, deep snow for much of the year, and poor weather conditions; 3) provide a redundant outflow to pair with the chosen primary outflow; and 4) reduce long-term outflow operation and maintenance. The tunnel was rapidly constructed to alleviate a pending disaster, and was not intended as a forever solution.   
From 2021-2024, the US Forest Service conducted public forums with stakeholders concerning the many outflow options for the Spirit Lake basin, before beginning the NEPA compliance process. The project requires that a primary outflow control measure be paired with a redundant outflow measure to create safety options. After two stakeholder meetings were held in July 2024, primary and redundant lake level control measures were analyzed and two were dismissed. The primary control measure dismissed from further consideration was an open channel outlet through the debris avalanche blockage that would almost entirely drain Spirit Lake. The dismissed redundant measure was a permanent emergency pumping station to be used during storm events or during maintenance of the primary outlet.

Nine potential outflow alternatives were identified. Some alternatives are primary outflow control measures, some are redundant control measures, and alternative 2 could be either.

The updated Draft Environmental Impact Statement (DEIS) identified the following two preferred alternatives…
• Alternative 2 - Full repair and rehabilitation of existing outlet tunnel as the primary outflow

• Alternative 7 - The phased construction and development of a natural habitat channel first requires a new

Details of the preferred alternatives… 
Alternative 2 (+9)  Full rehabilitation/maximum repair of the existing outflow tunnel, including enlarging the 
constricted JKL shear zone complex to restore full tunnel outflow capacity to 550 cubic feet per second. The 
intake of the existing tunnel would be lowered to 3,400 feet to allow operating the lake at a lower level. 
During future repairs to the tunnel when Lake Storage is the redundant measure, siphons installed at the intake structure would remain in place at the intake structure after construction. Permanent access would be provided by the Truman Trail Access Road.
Alternative 2 is not responsive to the voiced community importance placed on healthy ecosystems, including
restoration of the natural connection between Spirit Lake and the North Fork Toutle River for volitional fish
passage. This alternative forestalls safe development of a natural habitat channel in the future because it does
not include a pressure tunnel component, which is necessary for safe initiation of breaching the debris blockage. 
However, Alternative 2 limits loss of long-term research plots because it follows the path of the existing Truman Trail Access Road and has minor impacts to recreation.

Alternative 7 (+5/3/9) 
Alternative 7 is a phased approach to creating an outflow channel and restoring the natural connection between 
Spirit Lake and the North Fork Toutle River, and providing aquatic habitat and a volitional fish passage. 
The phased approach would begin with drawing down Spirit Lake’s water surface level with siphons. A new pressure tunnel (Alternative 5) would then be bored parallel to the existing tunnel under Harry’s Ridge, with a lowered intake at 3400 feet to provide additional lake storage (Alternative 9). The existing tunnel would be rehabilitated through the JKL shear zone (Alternative 3), and would be used to access the new tunnel intake for routine inspections and maintenance. The pressure tunnel would be the primary outflow measure, with the existing tunnel and lake storage each serving as a redundant outflow measure.    
The pressure tunnel and lowered lake level would allow a controlled partial breaching of the debris avalanche blockage through a combination of pilot channels. Controlled lake level modulation would hopefully prompt the outflow to establish a natural channel. Lower lake levels would be maintained until channels through the debris blockage are deemed naturally stable and can also function as a lake outflow redundant measure. The Truman Trail Access Road would be used to access the new outflow channel for inspections and maintenance.
