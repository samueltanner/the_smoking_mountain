Day 88     June 15, 1980  Sunday
USGS daily report
Weather conditions today permitted detailed observations of the crater's interior for the first time since the June 12th eruption, and the presence of the dome shown on radar images last Friday was confirmed visually. The dome was seen first this morning by observers in a Forest Service airplane and later from a helicopter at crater level. The dome is a symmetrical mass, circular in area, about 700 feet in diameter and rising 130 feet above the level of the crater floor. It is tan to light gray, and its surface is cracked into a pattern resembling large-scale bread crust texture. A slight red glow emanates from within the hot dome through cracks in the crust of solidified lava. Almost its entire margin was fuming today, as was much of the crater floor near the steeper crater walls. Small steam explosions, concentrated mainly at the northeastern edge of the dome, were periodically spouting small amounts of gray ash up to heights of about 300 to 700 feet within the crater. A shallow moat-like depression lies between the dome and the rampart to the north. The dome is the cooling top of a column of magma rising from beneath the volcano. However, no flows or other obvious signs of rapid growth were seen today. The record of Mount St. Helens' previous eruptions shows many highly explosive eruptive sequences within long-term eruptive episodes. Highly explosive eruptions of both tephra and gas could reach into the stratosphere, and pyroclastic flows could speed down the volcano flanks into adjacent valleys. Although the dome may cap the present volcanic vent, it could be blown away by eruptions, or explosions from beneath could direct lateral blasts down the slopes at high speeds. University of Washington seismographs recorded vibrations caused when sections of the crater rim avalanched back into the crater; low-level harmonic tremor also was recorded, but there were no actual earthquakes. The Spirit Lake Yacht Club
Spirit Lake’s water surface level was now 206 feet higher than before the eruption, rising from 3198 feet pre-eruption to 3404 feet. The lake’s surface area was 40 percent larger. 
The USGS Water Resources Division Tacoma office began studying Spirit Lake immediately. On May 19, 1980, one day after the eruption, the water temperature at the lake surface was 91ºF.  Spirit Lake’s temperature in May was historically about 50ºF.  Extreme heating and the introduction of avalanche debris and organic compounds caused Spirit Lake to immediately become anoxic (devoid of dissolved oxygen). A Secchi-disk water clarity measurement of one inch was taken days after the eruption. The color of the water was nearly black, largely due to the organic compounds and millions of pyrolyzed, floating trees.
[The two preceding photographs neatly match and illustrate Norm Dion’s story on the following slide…]

The June 1, 1980, USGS daily report read: 
The month has opened quietly. The volcano continues to vent steam, which rises in clouds to about 12,000 feet. Poor weather has prevented good aerial observation, but field crews, transported by helicopters, continued making ground observations and deploying monitoring instruments today. Measurements of the "new" Spirit Lake revealed that its depth is now about 100 feet or less, instead of its former depth of about 200 feet. The lake-water temperature was measured at 97°F (36 °C) at the surface and 95ºF (35°C) on the bottom. [the Daily Report quoted erroneous water temperatures…the June 1, 1980 lake temperature was 85º]   [USGS PP 1249]

Norm and Leonard Nelson had taken those sporadic depth soundings in the East Arm on that day just two weeks after the eruption. The soundings were markedly shallower than the former lake, but the volume was still completely unknown. 
Norm told the following story about that first boat venture onto Spirit Lake...
“Leonard and I were tasked with doing a bathymetric map of the new Spirit Lake. I asked Leonard if he'd work the fathometer, which he had a great deal of experience with, while I ran the boat and motor. When we got there and got out of the helicopter the gases coming off the lake gave us both terrible and immediate headaches. I was really feeling it, so as soon as we launched the boat and equipment, Leonard jumped to the stern, grabbed the motor, and said ‘OK, Norm, you run the fathometer’. Guess he saw that I needed some on the job training. A skill I never had another opportunity to use. But at least we somehow got the map started!”  
In summary, a current meter, attached to a recording fathomer, all depending on a constant speed from point to point, based on aerial photos. What could go wrong?
Today you could map the whole lake in two hours using GPS.”  - Norm Dion

Spirit Lake lost about 14,000 acre feet of water and the water surface level dropped from 3404 to 3400 between the eruption date and the end of July, due to its dry inlets and high water temperature causing increased evaporation. The water temperature measured 78º-81ºF on June 8, and 77ºF less than a foot below the surface on June 30.
 

On an early September 1980 sampling visit, Sandy Embrey and Norm observed large quantities of gas emitting from the lake surface, some of it in “boils” 2-3 feet in diameter. Fifty different gases were identified, and many were toxic and some flammable. The lake was completely anoxic (devoid of oxygen) through October 1980 in all three lake layers, the epilimnion (top layer), metalimnion (middle), and hypolimnion (bottom). The water temperature had dropped to 54ºF on October 16th, 1980, close to the temperature recorded in pre-eruption Spirit Lake in fall 1974. The lake clarity had also improved by fall 1980, with a Secchi-disk measurement of 2.5 feet. 
October 16, 1980. That was the day Sandy decided to go boating on Venus and Spirit Lake. Here’s the story about her day that was almost way too exciting, along with eight, great, never-seen-before photographs.
Pumiceous Pyroclastic Flows

We soon began calling ourselves "The Spirit Lake Yacht Club”. Sandy and Norm were charged with authoring two future reports on the water quality/chemical analysis (see references at end). Most of the sampling for their study had to be performed over the same spot in the deepest portions of the lake, meaning they had to venture out into the log raft. 
Getting a boat out onto the log-covered lake was challenging at best. The charred log raft consisted of wood ranging in size from huge trees to little pieces of bark. The shoreline was a land of mud, and dragging our aluminum boat and the required small mountain of equipment was often comical when our feet started sinking into the ooze. Two of the district chiefs, P.J. Carpenter and Bill Meyer, were authoring the study of the filling of Spirit Lake. Our section of the office, under Charley Swift, was fortunately doing the field work and bathymetric mapping.
Typical winter rain storms brought heavy precipitation to Mount St. Helens and Spirit Lake, and runoff raised the water level of the big lake 11 feet between November 1, 1980 and the beginning of March 1981. The water chemistry crew found that the autumnal lake water turnover had reoxygenated the top epilimnion layer to a small extent. The runoff diluted the chemically enriched water and concentrations of ionic constituents and bacteria had diminished greatly since the summer of 1980.

Bathymetric Survey
As spring began in March 1981, Leonard and I restarted the bathymetric survey of Spirit Lake on lucky days when the floating logs allowed running cross-sections by boat. We used the trisponder distance-ranger employed at Coldwater Lake, with the two remote units sending distance measurements to the master unit in the boat. At chosen intervals, I could “mark” the fathometer depth chart and later triangulate the two distances and the depth at that point and draw the bathymetric map. Before getting in the boat, we would have to establish solid reference marks under the remote unit tripods and well above the rising lake. We would then run elevation levels down to the water surface, and then fly cross lake to measure the distance and bearing between the two remotes. The reference marks were later tied to elevation datum, and northings/eastings were surveyed so the new map of the new lake was oriented correctly.
We completed most of the East Arm first, since the afternoon winds regularly pushed the massive log raft to the west. The East Arm was 190 feet deep before the eruption, the deepest part of the lake. After the arm was buried by up to 320 feet of debris avalanche material during the landslide, the maximum depth on that side of the lake was now 95 feet (early May 1981). Finishing the bathymetric mapping was very frustrating. More often than not, the log cover made mapping impossible. Our helicopter was frequently delayed by lowland fog at their base in Scappoose, Oregon, and by the time Leonard or I flew with the pilot to check on the log position the wind had normally shifted and we would have to pack up and drive back home. On a couple early trips, the conditions were perfect. After completing the bulk of our work, Leonard would shut off the outboard engine and we would float and enjoy lunch in a spectacular place. 

The massive log mat still contains large numbers of erect stumps which are floating either vertical or near vertical, usually tipped up by their intact rootwad. The erect stumps are submerged to varying degrees, from sticking above the water surface by several meters to barely submerged below the surface. Many are resting deep below the surface and solidly on the lake bottom, while others are lightly grounded and move with the wind. Using side-scan sonar a decade after the blast, researchers surveyed a transect covering only 1% of the lake bottom area. Even in such a small area, the sonar showed 154 vertical stumps and 95 prostrate logs. 
One fine day while mapping, I accidentally sat on the same side of the boat as Captain Leonard and obstructed his view. He was piloting the boat while I ran the trisponder and fathometer. We were slowly running a Spirit Lake cross-section (5 m.p.h.) when we ran directly into a vertical floating log. We came to an instant, jarring stop and all the expensive, delicate gear went sliding off the bench. I somehow caught the beautiful fathometer before it crashed onto aluminum. We were shocked, but luckily nothing was damaged. We laughed and resumed mapping, with me on the other side of the bench.

Finally, on May 5, 1981, the majority of the West Arm opened and we could complete the puzzle. In the middle of the arm, we measured a maximum depth of 112 feet. We started jumping around a bit in the boat...the West Arm was now the deepest part of Spirit Lake! Truly a new lake (see the following pre- and post-eruption bathymetric maps). On the date of the West Arm survey, the water surface level was 3420 feet in elevation, meaning the elevation of the lake bottom was 3308 feet and the volume of the lake was 166,600 acre-feet.
At the head of the West Arm is Bear Cove, where Bear Creek flows from Saint Helens Lake steeply down into Spirit Lake. As we motored into the cove, Leonard and I both dropped our jaws in disbelief and Leonard cut the engine. All around us were debris avalanche hummocks, eroded into sharp pinnacles by the sloshing lake water during the eruption. Some hummocks had blasted logs stranded on their top or side. The hummocks were streaked with colors of yellow, purple, orange, green, red, and black. We thought we were on the moon and looked around in stunned silence.  
I remember the day so clearly, like it was yesterday.

Our post-eruption bathymetric map (†1) of the new Spirit Lake debuted in Filling of Spirit Lake, Washington, May 18, 1980 to July 31, 1982  USGS Open-File Report 82-771, authored by two of our district chiefs, Philip J. Carpenter and William Meyer. The bathymetric map showed the 110-foot depth (112’) measured by Leonard and I in the West Arm on May 5, 1981, but did not indicate the 3420-foot surface water level on that day or the elevation of the lake bottom.
Charley Swift and Dave Kresch corrected that omission with an updated map (†2) and text in USGS Water-Resources Investigations Report 91-4028. Their updated map used lake bottom elevations instead of depth, showing 3310 feet in the West Arm and 3325 feet in the East Arm. This enabled the correct depth to be easily calculated as the surface water level changed due to filling or drawdown. 
The depth of Spirit Lake is still erroneously stated as 110 feet in many places on the Internet, including Wikipedia, even though Wikipedia includes Report 91-4028 in a list of Spirit Lake references. The northing/easting coordinates were also resurveyed, and this corrected the orientation of the East Arm.

I reworked the map to show both depth and lake bottom elevations (†3), using 3445 as the water surface level. Since the outlet tunnel was opened in May 1985, the lake level has normally been maintained between 3440 and 3445 feet in elevation.
Map †4 is from Alternative Strategies for a Permanent Outlet for Spirit Lake, Draft Environmental Impact Statement, U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, figure 6 page 11. The EIS states the map was based on 1983 surveys, but it looks exactly like our map changed to meters. The Corps of Engineer’s bathymetry uses a surface water level of 3463, the level maintained while the lake was stabilized by pumping from November 1982 - May 1985.
