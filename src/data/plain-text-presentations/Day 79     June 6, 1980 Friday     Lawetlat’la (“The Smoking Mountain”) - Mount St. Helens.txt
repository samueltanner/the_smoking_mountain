Day 79     June 6, 1980  Friday

The following photograph of the Plains of Abraham is a bit eerie, but I like the tiny bit of lighting. In the very center of the photo is a grove of trees that was seared and killed standing on the lee slope of the ridgeline. The silvered ghost forest is beautiful now. The braided stream in the lower right of the 1980 photo was our last night’s campsite on a hot circuit of the volcano seven summers ago. The streams draining the Plains of Abraham are the headwaters of Smith Creek, which was wildly affected by the pyroclastic density current, high-velocity scouring lahars, and blast pyroclastic flows from the May 18 blast.

Later Mount St. Helens researchers left a great deal of garbage behind at their various plots at the Plains of Abraham. Michele and I found metal stakes and cans, wood, plastic tubing, chains, and the curious-looking ladder apparatus. I decided to come back with zip ties at a later date and haul the eyesore blue plastic tubing out.
Two summers later, I made pretty quick work out of coiling the tubing in order to fasten it to my backpack. Other hikers and bikers gave me some puzzled glances as we passed along the trail. A few asked about my funny load and gave a thumbs up for hauling it out.
As I was driving home on Highway 12, a bunch of red ants were suddenly crawling on me. A few moments passed before I realized the six-legged hitchhikers were escaping from the tubing. And some Mount St Helens ash slurry was also dumping out, luckily into a rubber cargo tray in back. I later evicted the ants from the car, but the slurry stain still discolors the cargo tray to this day.


USGS daily report
Seismic activity remained low, and plumes consisting mostly of steam rose only about 15,000 feet above sea level. Loggers and property owners "swamped" State offices today seeking permits to get into the Red Zone, as Governor Ray's most recent executive order allows. Emission of S02 has increased significantly between June 3 and today. Today's measurement of about 900 tons a day is nearly four times greater than the June 3 measurements. Researchers from the National Center for Disease Control who analyzed four samples of volcanic ash for free silica found concentrations to be low-about 6 percent of the respirable size (less than 10 microns) by weight. Of this, two-thirds was in the form of the mineral cristobalite, and one-third was quartz. (Some chemical analyses previously had reported a high percentage of silica in the ash; most silica, however, was chemically bound in various other minerals, and it was not the dangerous "free silica," or Si02 .) Although exposure to free silica for many years can cause silicosis and exposure to large concentrations for a short time can cause an acute form of the disease, Federal health scientists do not think that short-term exposure to the ash is a significant public health hazard. They suggest that persons working in the ash, as well as those having respiratory diseases, should wear dust masks. Children should be cautioned not to play strenuously in ash-filled air, and they should not be allowed to play in deep ash. Tourists, the lifeblood of Washington's ocean beach communities, are "staying away in droves" after the traffic jams and accidents, strandings, and other problems that ashfall from the May 25 eruption created for Memorial Day travelers. The Ocean Shores Chamber of Commerce, according to the Tacoma News Tribune, reports that business in that normally busy resort community is "down 60 to 70 percent." One huge industrial vacuum cleaner was operated for 2 days to remove 17 tons of volcanic ash from the roof of the Yakima City Hall.  [USGS PP 1249]

Lahars also immediately swept down Smith Creek, Ape Canyon Creek, and the Muddy River on the east slope of the volcano, Pine Creek on the southeast flank, and Swift Creek on the south side. Within two minutes of the eruption, a pyroclastic surge boiled over the new eastern crater rim and was funneled down the Shoestring Glacier. A thirty-foot depth of the Shoestring Glacier’s snow and ice melted immediately, creating a massive lahar. The Muddy River lahar sped downslope at incredible speed, surged out of its channel and climbed a hill 245 feet above the pre-eruption riverbed. The lahar left its channel again just downstream and superelevated a second time. The Ape Canyon Creek lahar created a spectacular 260-foot runup of volcanic debris on Smith Creek’s eastern valley wall, opposite the confluence with Ape Canyon Creek. A superelevation of this magnitude required velocities of 90 m.p.h.  
These high-velocity lahars completely scoured their stream valleys, tearing away most trees and everything in their channel and leaving little deposition until far downstream. The lowermost reach of the Muddy River valley, where it enters Swift Reservoir, was completely choked with logs for one mile. There was major concern prior to the eruption about the potential of a catastrophic overtopping and breach of Swift Reservoir due to a large lahar/mudflow. Built in 1958, Swift Dam was one of the highest earthen dams in the world at time of construction. The reservoir pool elevation was eventually lowered 30 feet before the eruption. The reservoir water surface level rose 2.6 feet on May 18, and Swift Reservoir is huge.
