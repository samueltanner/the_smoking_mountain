Day 14     April 2, 1980  Wednesday
USGS daily report
A stronger burst of harmonic tremor was recorded this morning by many seismographs in the Mount St. Helens area between 0735 and 0750 hours. This burst confirmed the recognition of harmonic tremor yesterday and is the best indication to date that magma is moving beneath the mountain. The number of earthquakes greater than magnitude 3.0 increased to 63 today, reversing the decreasing trend of the past three days. Five of the quakes were stronger than magnitude 4.0; one was 4.8, among the strongest of the volcanic sequence to date [Today was to be the most active day, for earthquakes stronger than magnitude 3.0, during the entire month of April]. At least a dozen major eruption plumes shot above the mountain, one reaching an altitude of more than 20,000 feet. Large boulders and blocks of rock and ice were thrown out of the crater during one 30-minute eruption in mid-afternoon. A light ashfall left small amounts of ash on automobiles in Vancouver and Portland. The Forest Service's Pine Creek Ranger Station, southeast of the volcano, was closed permanently today after being evacuated on March 27. Asked about the likelihood of an explosive eruption - one that would hurl rock and lava from the mountain - Donal Mullineaux replied that it was the least likely of the volcano's possible actions. "A series of small eruptions would be more likely than a large, cataclysmic event," he said.  [USGS PP 1249]

Historic photographs
After Harry Truman’s very difficult personality destroyed their partnership in establishing the Mt. St. Helens Lodge, Jack and Tressa Nelson moved across the lake and built the quaint and peaceful Harmony Falls Lodge on Spirit Lake’s East Arm. They built Harmony Falls in 1931, and owned and operated the Lodge with Tressa’s sister Ruby until the 1960s. Guests arriving across the lake could hike a few miles around the East Arm, or use an ancient hand-cranked telephone to announce their arrival. Jack would pilot two steel-hulled Coast Guard boats to retrieve the guests and ferry them back across the lake, with views of Mount St. Helens’ towering glaciers getting bigger along the way. The boats were christened the Tressa and the Ruby.
Many of their guests returned year after year to the eleven cabins with wood stoves, oil lamps, creaky beds, and rustic charm. Tressa and Ruby were famous in the lodge kitchen, and their huckleberry pies were legendary. They were able to keep all of their goods perfectly cooled at the base of Harmony Falls’ 100+ foot cascade, a steep but short climb behind the lodge. The falls drove a Pelton wheel to electrify the lodge.
