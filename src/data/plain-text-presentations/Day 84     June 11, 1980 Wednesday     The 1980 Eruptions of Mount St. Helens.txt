South Fork Castle Creek Lake  
Blockage, Filling, Study and Concern about Breaching, Bathymetries, and Construction of Spillway

The Marginal geologic unit of the Debris Avalanche was part of the first block of the massive rockslide that swept down Mount. St. Helens’ northern flank. The Marginal unit immediately mixed with melted snow from the volcano and water in the upper North Fork Toutle River and flowed down valley. This more saturated unit was then pushed along and aside by the massive North Fork Unit from the second landslide block. The Marginal unit was pushed 800 feet up South Fork Castle Creek valley, primarily on the valley's eastern side. Almost directly north across the North Fork Toutle River valley, the Marginal unit was shoved 8650 feet (1.6 miles) up Coldwater Creek valley.
The debris avalanche created a blockage across the South Fork Castle Creek valley (5.5 miles WNW of Mount St. Helens), and a lake immediately began forming. The debris dam blocking S.F. Castle Creek valley was 2000 feet wide. The crest of the blockage was at 2683 feet in elevation at the western end, and 2590 feet at the eastern end. The pre-eruption channel of S.F. Castle Creek flowed on the valley’s western side and directly beneath Point 2683, and the blockage reached its greatest thickness there (263 feet). A large, mile-long blast pyroclastic flow was emplaced in lower South Fork Castle Creek valley by the lateral pyroclastic surge.
South Fork Castle Creek Marsh occupied the valley pre-eruption and was very large, and more than a mile long at 2480-2560 feet in elevation. Researchers believe a prehistoric lake had also been impounded behind thick pyroclastic flows or lahars, and the valley floor slowly filled with alluvium. This lake probably overtopped the prehistoric blockage, incising a deep pre-1980 channel downstream of the flat marsh through 2500-year-old+ 

deposits. The pre-1980 valley once contained many exceptionally large trees, but the surrounding ridges and the head of the valley had been extensively logged.
Some researchers referred to the new lake as "West Castle Lake", and the nomenclature among researchers was varied. The lake is now referred to as Castle Lake but is truly in South Fork Castle Creek valley. Castle Creek occupies the valley just to the east.

The stability of the blockage and the potential for a catastrophic breach and flood was of great concern. Numerous seeps from the dam with substantial discharge toward the toe of the blockage were observed and measured beginning in 1980. Water was also observed discharging from the dam towards the lake. Discharge at various elevations and in different directions is uncharacteristic of a typical earthen dam, which normally discharges along the base at the toe of the dam. John Cummans, Leonard Nelson and I surveyed the low overflow point of the blockage at 2578.29 feet on April 23, 1981 and determined the fast-growing lake’s surface was only 39 feet below the lowest point in the debris dam. The lake was predicted to overtop the blockage by late winter or early spring 1982.

The Corps of Engineers reclaimed an old logging road to gain vehicle access to the low end of the avalanche debris blockage crest at South Fork Castle Lake in August 1981. Piezometers were installed to measure groundwater levels in the blockage. Large groundwater mounds were discovered at elevations both above and below the water surface level of the lake. The US Army Corps of Engineers and the US Geological Survey weighed drawing South Fork Castle Lake down 40 feet or entirely, artificially draining the groundwater mounds at the crest of the blockage, or creating a spillway outlet. The cost of entirely draining the lake was enormous, and a spillway was chosen.
The Corps of Engineers completed excavation and construction in late October 1981 of a reinforced, unlined, uncontrolled spillway that flows from the lake's northeastern end. The spillway is 885 feet long and 100 feet wide and is designed for 2600 cubic feet per second (cfs) at flood stage. The banks are reinforced with riprap but the bed of the spillway is not. The channel was designed to be down cut by about 15 feet, effectively reducing surface elevation, volume, and risk over time.

A bathymetric survey was performed and a map was drawn by the Tacoma USGS in November 1981. South Fork Castle Lake had a surface area of 280 acres, 17,500 acre-foot volume, and a maximum depth of 120 feet. Sandy Embrey and Norm Dion performed chemical analyses on South Fork Creek Castle Lake from May 1981 until August 1982. The clarity was measured at a four-foot depth by Secchi-disk in May 1981. The clarity improved to 11 feet by August 1982. They calculated the lake's water exchange time at 1.3 years. 
The bathymetry was questioned after the 1981-1982 water quality/chemical analyses and further depth measurements of the lake. The valley was formerly a flat-bottomed marsh and the middle was not incised by a creek either pre-or post-eruption. The 120-foot depth may have been an erroneous reading (see 1981 bathymetry on slide 44). 
Concern regarding the accuracy of the original elevation datum also arose during subsequent studies of the blockage, and a 1981 contour map (prepared by Tallany, Van Kuren, Gertis, and Thielman under contract with the USGS) was subject to a high degree of uncertainty owing to a problem with the vertical and horizontal control. The map was referenced to the arbitrary local elevation datum, which the USGS adjusted by approximately 5.8 meters (19 feet) to a geodetic elevation datum in 1983.
To ascertain the volume of water held behind the marginally stable blockage, a 2017 USGS study further corrected the datum issues and performed a new bathymetry with more sensitive instruments than were available in 1981. South Fork Castle Lake’s maximum depth was found to be 98 feet. A constant-recording gage was installed by the USGS in October 1993 on the right bank of the lake's outflow. The water surface level averages 2579 feet in elevation. The maximum level recorded was 2582.76 feet on February 8, 1996, and the minimum was 2578.31 feet on September 5-7, 2003.
