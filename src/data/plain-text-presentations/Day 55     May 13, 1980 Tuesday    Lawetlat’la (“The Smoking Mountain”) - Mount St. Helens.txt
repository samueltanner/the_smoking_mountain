Day 55     May 13, 1980  Tuesday

USGS daily report
Steam and ash continue to erupt, but the rate slackened in the afternoon. The eruption cloud rose only about 3,000 feet above the crater, and most of its ash fell nearby. However, a false report (of unknown origin) that a "mammoth" eruption had sent ash as high as 18,000 feet above sea level caused the Federal Aviation Administration to issue a pilots' warning that an ash-plume area extended 20 miles north-northeast of the volcano and was "of extreme hazard to aircraft." Scientists at Vancouver spent much of today quashing rumors about the exaggerated size of the ash plume and about a nonexistent lava flow, which was reported to have been moving down the side of the volcano.

The pit at the head of Shoestring Glacier has enlarged, and steam emission from that site has increased. USGS observers also reported that new steaming areas have opened on the western side of the mountaintop, just outside the crater rim. Geologists noted yellow-green encrustations on the old ash on the upper southern slopes of the volcano, probably from sulfur that was either deposited by the fumes or leached from the ash. Similar encrustations have been seen for weeks within the crater and around the rim. Earthquake activity continued at a lesser rate; 21 events stronger than magnitude 3.0 were recorded, including six of magnitude 4 or greater.     [USGS PP 1249]

Historic Photograph
Remember the story from April 3, 1980 about Fred Miller of Skykomish? He was the “longhair in beret and backpack” who had climbed to see the volcano’s new craters, had a 13-mile-long trudge down from Timberline, and was picked up by USGS geologist Rick Hoblitt and fed dinner in Castle Rock as he told his story.

Robert Rogers is another great story. On April 24, Rogers headed up the west flank of Mount St. Helens for the second time since the volcano became active. Exhausted at 4 a.m., he unrolled a bag and wrapped up in a grey poncho for camouflage against ashy snow. A helicopter’s whine bolted him awake hours later in dazzling sun. 
The Bell Jet Ranger landed nearby and two men climbed out. Rogers grabbed his gear and his ice axe, ready for a dash downslope. 
“Do you arrest trespassers?” Rogers asked. 
“No, we’re scientists.” 
The USGS scientists were staking out black-plastic-covered plywood targets, one of several control points around the volcano for aerial photographs. They had one more to establish on the summit and they told Rogers to jump in. “Come on up!”
On the summit, Rogers wandered, taking pictures of great ice cracks, the gaping crater, and deep alternating layers of ash and snow above blue glacier ice. The scientists finished their target, the helicopter flew off and returned near noontime. David Johnston climbed out, took some photographs of the climber on the summit, and then Rogers bolted down the slope in the tracks of giant rolled rocks.  



Robert Rogers would be back on the west flank on May 17 and 18 with his friend Francisco Valenzuela and live to tell even bigger tales. He took a set of very famous photographs of Mount St. Helens’ massive landslide and the initial seconds of the big eruption from the south side of the South Fork Toutle River.   [Richard Waitt  USGS] 

Today’s historic photograph is a great black and white by Donal Mullineaux of the west side of Mount St. Helens. Robert Rogers probably chose this side since it was so quiet, and a maze of logging roads led almost to timberline.

Here’s the short story about Fred Miller in case you missed it…

Driving down the Spirit Lake Highway, Rick Hoblitt of the USGS stopped for a longhair in beret and backpack. Young Fred Miller caked in ash. He’d hitchhiked from Snohomish on April Fool’s Day 1980, thumbed a ride up the South Fork Toutle River and climbed to treeline on April 2. Miller trudged a long spiral to the summit. Still two craters, he reported, ice at their rims and capped by fifteen feet of new rock. Rotten-egg gas wafted up from crevasses, and ash doused him on Dog’s Head. He had hiked thirteen miles down from Timberline when Hoblitt picked him up and bought him dinner in Castle Rock.
