Day 78     June 5, 1980  Thursday    

Here's Rodney Williams, USGS hydrologist and Information Officer at the Tacoma Water Resources Division office. You met Rod yesterday, surveying with Jimmy Poole from the giant Muddy River Boulder.
I have known Rod my entire life, growing up in the same church that Rod, Annette, and their children attended. Rod hired me as a high school senior to help with a small basins project, since he knew I was both map and mountain crazy. Plus, if I was even half as smart as my older cousin Tom, who also worked college summers with the Survey, I would probably work out. 
Lots of people at the Tacoma office referred to Rod as "Mr. Nice Guy", and he wore it well. Choosing him to be the Information Officer and the first point of contact for the public was pure genius. Rod and three workmates played cards in the office library during most lunch breaks and the others were all great guys, just like Rod.
Rod and I had been exchanging letters, excited to attend a summer Retiree's breakfast together in 2019, and I was all set to pick him up on the second Tuesday in July. Sadly, he and Annette passed away within two months of each other that spring at the age of 89.

USGS daily report
Eruptive and seismic activity at Mount St. Helens remained low today. USGS and University of Washington scientists have concluded that the ominous pattern of earthquakes and harmonic tremor earlier this week, which had been thought to indicate an impending eruption, "must be considered a false alarm." 
Washington State University scientists in Pullman have determined, based on experimental work with chickens, that the ash is not acutely toxic to domestic animals. Scientists are still concerned, however, that eating and breathing ash will have some effect on larger animals. The Coast Guard office in Portland reports that dredging of the Columbia River shoal has deepened the navigation channel to 33 feet, more than three times deeper than the 10-foot depth left after the May 18th/19th floods from the Cowlitz River. Colleagues of photographer Blackburn, who died in the May 18 eruption, found one of the remote-controlled cameras that he had been operating from Coldwater I observation station. Film from the battered camera was processed, but it had been fogged by the heat and showed no clear images of the May 18th blast.   [USGS PP 1249]

The volcanic lahars that swept down the South Fork Toutle River valley and its tributary Sheep Canyon Creek began minutes after the initial eruption on May 18 beginning at 0832 hours. Steep gradient and rapid melting of the Toutle and Talus Glaciers’ snow and ice caused the South Fork Toutle lahar to travel at a much higher average velocity (15mph) than the massive mudflow which began 4-1/2 hours later in the North Fork Toutle River valley. Most mudflows occurred on the volcano’s drainages before midday on May 18, save for the major mudflow on the North Fork Toutle. Velocities and length-descent ratios for the May 18 lahars generally fall within the range recorded for earlier lahars at Mount St. Helens and at comparable volcanoes. The long runout of the main lahar on the North Fork Toutle River, however, is somewhat anomalous.

The high velocity of the South Fork Toutle created a superelevation, pushing the lahar an astonishing 360 feet up the river’s north bank near to where the toe of Crescent Ridge meets the river. The mudflow mainly scoured the steep river valley at that elevation (3600-4000 feet) and left little or no deposition. The lahar superelevated again about a half mile further downstream near the confluence of Sheep Canyon Creek, running 180 feet up the north bank. Lahars  
Part I

The South Fork Toutle River lahar was intense but of short duration. At 1003 hours, a deputy on flood watch radioed that "a 5-foot rise of the South Toutle River" was passing Weyerhaeuser's Camp 12. At 1005, the flow was "knocking down trees," and at 1007 a "12-foot flash flood" containing logs, debris, and buildings was coming downstream from Camp 12 toward the Harry Gardner Bridge.  
The lahar arrived at the South Fork’s confluence with the North Fork Toutle River (25 miles from the volcano) at 1020 hours. The lahar nearly touched the underside of the Coal Bank Bridge over Spirit Lake Highway 504. Both the Harry Gardner and Coal Bank Bridges survived the South Fork mudflow. Toutle residents described the mudflow front as a tangle of logs and other woody debris 8-10 feet high that advanced downstream at 12 feet per second. 
Peak discharge of the lahar was hand measured at the destroyed stage recorder at the Main Branch Toutle River gaging station at Silver Lake. The high-water mark at Silver Lake was a foot higher than the record flow at a station with 70 years of discharge data. A second, smaller lahar came down the South Fork Toutle at 1400, after part of the Plinian eruption column collapsed over Mount St. Helens’ new crater rim and sent a pyroclastic flow down onto the two glaciers which are the source of the river.

Three months after the May 18, 1980 eruption, my three cousins and I set out to study the Sheep Canyon Creek lahar. Robert Sudduth, Tom Drake, and Elizabeth Burke piled into my parent’s 1966 VW Bug and we drove the length of the beautiful Kalama River to the road end near the present-day Blue Lake trailhead. Surveying work and aerial photo control had brought me to the south side of Mount St. Helens several times that first summer after the cataclysmic eruption, and I had been on the road to the jersey-barrier roadblock before. Robert, Tom, Elizabeth, and I wandered past for about five miles on an easy grade on a cloudy day. The huckleberries were heavy on the bushes, having had no visitors to pick them here inside the Red Zone.   
The Forest Service road wound us through stands of trees that had survived the pyroclastic density current, by living on the south side of the South Fork Toutle River instead of the north side. We found the old parking lot for the trail to Sheep Canyon Creek, and seconds later burst out onto the steep and eerie deposit of the lahar. Sheep Canyon Creek had already deeply incised the lahar surface, and complete devastation was visible across the river on the north valley wall. The scorched trees at the confluence below had a strange orange hue. Across the valley, Road 4170 climbed from the valley floor and did a hairpin turn.  We did not know it at the time, but our vantage included the roadside camps where the Karr family and Robert Landsburg perished.
