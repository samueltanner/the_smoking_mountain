Day 69     May 27, 1980  Tuesday

I'm sure you thought I forgot to tell you what happened to Mary and Ron Rinear's North Fork cabin, and their buddy Blair Barner’s red A-Frame right next door. 
Mary and Ron and their children drove down to their cabin at Maple Flats (Kid Valley) on the North Fork of the Toutle River on Friday, May 16. They had originally planned to stay the whole weekend, but instead had to drive back to Lacey on Saturday for Ron to run a work-a-thon fundraiser for North Thurston High School girl’s athletic programs. By chance, the Rinear’s were not at their cabin Sunday morning May 18 as they had hoped when the weekend started out. They had little news except "someone said they think your cabin was destroyed". If not destroyed, they had been told that their cabin was probably full of mud. 
Mary and Ron were unable to get to their cabin until nine days after the eruption, due to severed bridges, deeply buried roads, and the second eruption on Sunday, May 25. They had to walk in from Kid Valley to their cabin, post holing through knee-deep goo in places. They were mistaken as potential looters by another resident and had to explain at length who they were. The North Fork mudflow on the 18th essentially buried the A-Frame cabin, and covered Mary and Ron’s foundation and front step and miraculously stopped there. The front of Blair’s A-Frame had been battered and broken by large logs, which were left inside on top of about 4 feet of mud.
Following are photographs of their cabin and the A-Frame, and several from around Mary and Ron's Maple Flats neighborhood. 
You'll meet Mary, Ron, and Blair again tomorrow and on June 12, as there is more to their Mount St. Helens story…

USGS daily report
Seismicity was very low during the day, and eruptions of mixed ash and steam were mild. USGS scientists examined ash from Sunday's eruptions and found it to be a mixture of pumice, glass shards, crystal fragments, and fragments of older rock- similar to the composition of the May 18 ash. Field crews saw younger pyroclastic flows on top of the May 18 deposits. The pumice in the newer deposits is of two types - one a light-colored, frothy rock and the other darker and more dense. As the ash from last Sunday's eruption dries out, motorists along Interstate Highway 5 find that problems with visibility and clogged air cleaners are increasing. The Washington State Patrol imposed an emergency speed limit of 25 miles an hour, but, according to reporter Kerry Webster of the Tacoma News Tribune, it was being ignored by so many impatient drivers that "dust clouds were spread for miles obscuring both sides of the highway." State Patrolmen were greatly hampered in enforcing the new regulation because speeders were creating dense "smokescreens" of ash dust. Finally, according to Webster, after a multi car accident had delayed northbound traffic for more than 1 hour, truck drivers took matters into their own hands. Communicating by citizen's band radios, they maneuvered their rigs into three-abreast formations and formed moving roadblocks traveling at the emergency speed limit. The trios of trucks spaced themselves about 7 miles apart, just about far enough for the dust from one convoy to settle before the next arrived. A State Patrol sergeant who was interviewed by Webster reported that the accident rate dropped immediately and that the dust began to clear. He added that, although the State Patrol did not condone the truckers' action, it certainly kept the highway open and probably saved some lives.   [USGS PP 1249]

The Debris Avalanche deposit in the North Fork Toutle River valley averaged 175 feet in depth. The maximum depth in the valley is approximately 590 feet, at a trio of hummocks 1.5 miles west of Spirit Lake’s present-day shoreline. The hummocks are shown as Point 3507 on new topographical maps, and the pre-eruption elevation of the North Fork Toutle River directly below was 2920 feet. Robert and Minnie Lange’s Spirit Lake Lodge was buried by 430 feet of debris at the toe of the depositional ramp, termed The Spillover. The avalanche climbed the ramp 1300 feet up and over South Coldwater Ridge and down into South Fork Coldwater Creek valley. Harry Truman’s Mt. St. Helens Lodge lies underneath about 240 feet of debris avalanche and 80 feet of Spirit Lake water.
Six hundred seventy-five (675) hummocks of debris were identified, and each was measured for volume, height, and width. The tallest hummock is 240 feet high and lies seven miles downstream from the volcano, near Coldwater Creek, and the longest (600 feet) is about nine miles from the mountain.
Temperatures of the debris avalanche were directly measured by thermocouple (an electrical temperature measuring device). Taken at depths of 3-5 feet 10-12 days after the eruption, temperatures still ranged from 154ºF. - 208ºF.
