Day 62     May 20, 1980  Tuesday   
Photographs of Mount St. Helens’ lakes and river valleys taken on this date 45 years ago...

• Flying over Saint Helens Lake and Coldwater Peak, seven miles due north of the vent and above Spirit Lake. †1
• Two photos from a low airplane pass over the radically-changed Spirit Lake. The Eagle Point peninsula divides the lake into East and West Arms. Hummocks from the debris avalanche can be seen steaming and smashed up against the peninsula, and other hummocks are exposed as islands. †2
• The many helicopter pilots contracting with the Geological Survey, Forest Service, Corps of Engineers, etc., were driven crazy by many fixed-wing aircraft flying illegally around the volcano. One fellow, known as Rocky from Toledo, regularly terrorized them. I remember riding towards Spirit Lake one morning and suddenly our pilot cursed as a plane’s wing shadow appeared close below us on the debris avalanche. Our pilot immediately whipped his head around in all directions, trying to figure out where it was. He said it was a fairly routine and maddening occurrence. †3
• The pyroclastic density current (lateral pyroclastic surge) raced down South Fork Coldwater Creek valley in the first 2-1/2 minutes of the eruption. A secondary deposit of superheated fine ash, termed the Blast Pyroclastic flow, stayed suspended and sifted down from ridges to pond in low elevation areas to depths up to 60 feet. Rick Hoblitt of the USGS is taking temperatures and examining the deposit, along with photographs of smoking, black fumaroles. †4
• The massive debris fan, scoured channels, and superelevation of the Muddy River and Pine Creek on Mount St. Helens' east flank, below the Shoestring Glacier. †5
• Remember driving across the Al Raught bridge over the North Fork Toutle River back in the April 24 post? They found a chunk of the green structure a quarter-mile downstream, carried there by the thick eruption day mudflow before the velocity slackened and the span was set back down. †6

USGS daily report
Sporadic eruptive activity is producing mostly steam. Seismicity, too, has declined to a level lower than any since March 22; no earthquakes registered more than magnitude 3 today. Scientists in the field are finding that the debris avalanche that rushed down the upper valley of the North Fork Toutle River immediately after the initial Sunday morning blast includes hot areas as well as cold ones. Scattered warm masses in the avalanche debris, which is largely the remains of the "bulge" mass from the mountain, probably came from hot parts of the volcano, and colder parts are blocks of ice from the destroyed glaciers that were caught in the flow. The debris avalanche deposit is riddled with phreatic explosion pits, and, now and then, scientists hear explosions, which occur when the buried heat in the deposit and subsurface water meet and flash into steam. Field crews also have measured the temperature of some of the pyroclastic flows that followed the initial blast. At a depth of about two feet, they obtained readings of nearly 300°F.    
No one yet knows how much of a flood threat the "new," higher Spirit Lake may be. One crucial unknown factor is the stability of the debris avalanche deposit that buried the lake's former outlet to North Fork Toutle River and impounded the lake at its higher level. Another uncertainty is whether the lake, now that its former outlet is blocked, will rise until it overtops the debris dam. Although most of the water displaced from the lake by last Sunday's giant avalanche already has drained back, the lake continues to receive inflow from its various tributary streams. If the dam is overtopped, Crandell said, "It will be like water going over a spillway; but, in this case, the spillway is not made of concrete, but of very unstable material." The spilling water could quickly erode a channel through the debris dam that would release more floodwaters, perhaps at catastrophic rates, to downstream areas.

Scientists who are watching Spirit lake (as best they can) report that the water level thus far has remained below the top of the debris dam, and no flooding from this source is imminent. They cannot measure the lake's level directly, and aerial observation of the deposits southwest of the lake, in the area that might be overtopped, is greatly hampered by steam pouring from dozens of fumaroles. However, aerial radar imagery has shown no signs that the area of the lake is enlarging, and some observers have seen water seeping through (or beneath) the debris dam and flowing westward. If this seepage keeps pace with inflow to the lake, the chances that the lake level will stabilize below the dam crest and that the dam itself will hold are greatly increased. If the dam does fail, it might go quickly and (if visibility were poor when it failed) with little warning. A flash-flood watch remains in effect along the Toutle River, and Washington State Patrol officers are stationed at the Interstate Highway 5 bridge across the Toutle River to close the highway again, if it becomes necessary. Hydrologists working with limited data are trying to estimate the likely height and speed of the flood crest that would occur if the dam is breached. On the basis of the available information, they cannot guarantee even a 1-hour warning for downstream areas. 
Existing maps of the Cowlitz River channel and flood plains are as obsolete as pre-eruption maps of the Spirit Lake area. Today, hydrologists began remapping the debris-choked channel in order to evaluate the continuing flood hazard. They are finding that the river bottom is as much as 15 feet shallower than it was before the mudflows and that the water-carrying capacity of the channel is greatly reduced.
Since the May 18 eruption, nearly continuous bad weather has prevented flying over the mountain to obtain accurate data on changes in heat emission. An aerial infrared survey flown by a U.S. Navy airplane at midday, however, and a daytime helicopter survey over the accessible western and southern flanks of Mount St. Helens 


revealed no new sources of volcanic heat emission outside the crater. The City of Yakima has declared itself a 
disaster area, and Washington Governor Dixie Lee Ray telegraphed President Carter to ask that the entire State be declared so. Many schools and roads are still closed, and the Columbia River remains closed to most shipping, but people are digging out of the ash.   [USGS PP 1249]
