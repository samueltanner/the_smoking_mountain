It’s time to change the focus to Mount St. Helens’ lakes…  

31 lakes were directly affected by the May 18, 1980 eruption, most by the pyroclastic density current (lateral pyroclastic surge). Spirit Lake was affected by both the pyroclastic surge and the debris avalanche and is essentially a new lake in its former basin. Coldwater Lake and South Fork Castle Lake are new lakes created when the two creek valleys were dammed by the debris avalanche in the North Fork Toutle River valley.  
Two small lakes (Bear and Hoo Hoo) on the southeast side of Mount St. Helens were completely covered by the lahars in the Smith Creek/Muddy River drainages. Several other lakes received heavy amounts of tephra but were technically unaffected and lie just outside the devastation zone (Deadmans, Island, O’Connor, Deer, and other small lakes north of the volcano).

Coldwater Lake’s rapid filling behind its new debris dam was a huge cause for concern, and the USGS Water Resources Division Tacoma office began bathymetric mapping there. The next dozen days will feature many presentations about the bathymetric mapping, water chemistry, filling, and spillway construction at Coldwater, South Fork Castle Creek, and Spirit Lakes. Presentations about Saint Helens Lake, Deadmans Lake, and Fawn Lake will follow, along with bathymetric maps and photographs of many other affected lakes. The presentations include stories told by members of the Spirit Lake Yacht Club about our adventures and misadventures while slinging a boat by helicopter into the vast devestation zone of downed trees and mud and ash to study the lakes.

Coldwater Lake 
The eruption of Mount St. Helens on May 18, 1980 created the largest landslide in recorded history. A debris avalanche containing 3.3 billion cubic yards of rock and ice swept 17 miles down the North Fork Toutle River valley, blocking Coldwater Creek's confluence with the river. The less-viscous (more fluid) Marginal geologic unit of the debris avalanche was pushed aside and up Coldwater valley by the massive and blocky North Fork unit that followed down valley within minutes. The Marginal unit surged 8650 feet (1.4 miles) up the valley and created a new, fast-growing lake behind the debris plug. Coldwater valley (7 miles NW of the volcano) and its surrounding ridges had been extensively logged prior to the eruption, and logging operations were active in May 1980. The remaining trees in the basin were completely blown down or snapped off.
I had gone back to college at Western Washington University in Bellingham as a junior in fall quarter 1980, but was missing way too much excitement at the volcano and decided that I better seize the moment. Flying to work and mapping Mount St. Helens’ lakes could not wait, but school could. Coldwater Lake was getting deep very rapidly. Coldwater valley is the second largest tributary to the North Fork Toutle River (after the Green River), and drains the steep slopes of Minnie Peak, Mount Whittier, Mount Margaret, The Dome, and Coldwater Peak. Note Coldwater valley’s constriction, labeled in the next photograph. The valley had a considerable drop in elevation from above the constriction to below, at least a 120-foot drop. Check out the huge amount of water that had backed up behind the debris blockage between that photo taken only days after the May 18 eruption and the photo taken January 12, 1981, one week before bathymetric mapping began. The lake is a couple feet deep in the first photo, 138 feet deep on January 12. By June 1981, the lake was all the way up and around the bend.

Coldwater Lake bathymetric survey
Leonard Nelson and I began making a bathymetric map (underwater depth contour) of Coldwater Lake on January 19, 1981. Leonard had mapped hundreds of lakes in Washington State and was an expert at his craft and a patient teacher. The winter of 1980/1981 brought little snow to the area surrounding Mount St. Helens. New snow quickly disappeared due to the rapid erosion/run-off of the blast deposits, and melting in places where the debris avalanche and lateral pyroclastic surge deposit were still slightly warm.
We were using some fancy and expensive new equipment...a Microwave Trisponder range finder that continually measured the distance from two remote units and was recorded by a master unit in our 16-foot aluminum boat. The master unit was connected to a Raytheon fathometer (depth recorder). When I “marked” the fathometer chart, we would know our distance from the right and left remotes and the depth of the lake at that spot. The Raytheon fathometer was cool. It was as big as a small suitcase and had a large roll of paper chart and stylus pens for recording depth profiles (see following photographs of the Raytheon fathometer and Trisponder). Normally, bathymetric surveys were done in conjunction with a high-resolution vertical aerial photograph. We would do many cross-sections of a lake with the fathometer, both width and length, and then create the underwater contour map in the office with the charts, engineer’s scale, and a large compass. However, the floating logs on Coldwater Lake made running straight cross-sections impossible. The Microwave Trisponder distance finder guaranteed accurate data, and it was truly state-of-the-art 1980s style. Today, with a decent Global Positioning System (GPS) unit, a fathometer, and a computer, you could have the bathymetric map partly done by the time you got out of the boat.

Leonard and I started by securely establishing Reference Points (RPs) directly under the tripods that held the remote units. We then surveyed down to the lake to determine the water surface level relative to each remote unit elevation. Establishing solid RPs was difficult since the surge deposit was up to a meter deep and so easily eroded. These Reference Points would later be tied to benchmarks that were being laboriously surveyed up the valleys into the blast zone. Most of the land surface was vastly changed, especially where the debris avalanche deposited material hundreds of feet in depth. Pre-eruption USGS elevation benchmarks were all destroyed, and all elevations were unknown or fuzzy. From a map maker’s or surveyor’s standpoint, all elevations were essentially “floating” until they could be tied to a known, established point.
Our work required a ton of equipment, often taking two trips from Maple Flats to the lakes. We had the 3 pieces of the Microwave Trisponder, the giant fathometer (and a back-up fathometer), a transducer for sending the sonar signal to the lake bottom, tripods, an automatic surveying level and fiberglass rod, 12 volt auto batteries for running the equipment, tools, flotation gear, radios, an outboard engine and gas tank...and our aluminum boat that was often slung under the helicopter up to the lakes. Plus, most of the gear was sensitive and fairly delicate and blowing ash got into everything. We were required to map the lake in portions due to poor weather and floating logs. On one occasion, we arrived at Coldwater to find the boat upside down, completely submerged in 3-4 feet of water and stuck securely to the muddy lake bottom. The lake had risen unexpectedly fast during winter rains and the boat had not been secured high enough. The drain plug had been removed and the boat flipped over, but this allowed the water to rise over the boat and created a very strong vacuum. We had to wade into the freezing water, and after many heave-hos, Leonard managed to get a fence post under the rail. 

We cranked on the post and finally the vacuum released with a whoosh. The boat popped up fast and we got out of the way quickly, just managing to grab the bow rope. Our workday was saved!
By March 10, 1981, we had enough data after a few trips to draw a preliminary map of Coldwater’s lake bottom. I drew the map on a huge poster board with a giant compass to triangulate, along with Leonard’s occasional expert guidance. The bathymetric was very difficult to draw, and the fathomer chart hard to interpret. The continually rising lake had covered up about a mile of the debris avalanche plug in the valley, and the now-submerged hummocks were hard to decipher and created many underwater miniature mountains and terraces...not the regular contours of most bathymetric maps. The maximum depth was measured just beyond the upvalley end of the debris blockage, at a point that was once the stream channel of Coldwater Creek. Since the water surface level of the lake was constantly rising, determining the bottom elevation was most important. On March 10, 1981, Coldwater Lake was 164 feet deep, with the surface at 2443 feet in elevation, and the lake bottom at 2279 feet. The lake was about 2.4 miles long.

The flood threat posed by the lake was immense, as Coldwater would eventually overtop the debris avalanche deposit if not partially drained. The same was true, on a smaller scale, at South Fork Castle Creek’s blocked tributary on the opposite side of the North Fork Toutle River valley. 
On April 23, 1981, I happily flew with our Surface Water Specialist, John Cummans, and Leonard to survey the low overflow points in the blockage at Coldwater Lake and at South Fork Castle Lake. Coldwater Lake had risen 13 feet in the five weeks since finishing our preliminary mapping in March. The surface water level of the lake was now 2456 feet in elevation, and the low overflow point determined to be at 2516 feet. Only 60 feet more before the big lake breached.

The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers bulldozed a road into Coldwater Lake from the Green River valley on May 12, 1981 and began construction of an uncontrolled spillway in mid-June. The spillway was dug into the bedrock toe of the ridge separating Coldwater and Maratta Creeks and was designed for 18,650 cfs (cubic feet per second) at the maximum predicted flood stage. As soon as the road into Coldwater was completed, a USGS drill rig was driven in, and we began drilling and installing piezometers in the debris blockage. A piezometer is a device that measures the depth of groundwater, which was found to be moving and ponding in the 200-320-foot-thick blockage at the mouth of Coldwater valley. Working around the drill rig could be exciting with enormous bulldozers, dump trucks and earth-scraping machines hurtling past with their loads at speeds up to 40 miles an hour as they excavated the spillway. We wore hard hats in the truck on our long drives to Coldwater since the road was so rough that we routinely hit our heads on the top of the truck cab. We did take time out for a little fun after long days drilling piezometer holes. I often had an outboard engine stashed in the back of the truck. Our battered aluminum boat was parked at Coldwater and we took some long afternoon adventures up the now-giant lake. All in the name of science, of course.

By June 1, Cold​water had risen to 2469 feet, and heavy rain in early June sent the water level to 2472 (a foot above the planned spillway elevation). Coldwater Lake had become a 190-foot-deep lake in one year’s time. If the lake had risen to the top of the blockage its volume would have been 105,000 acre-feet (an acre-foot is equal to 325,000 gallons; the amount of water needed to cover an acre of ground to a depth of one foot). A breach of the debris avalanche dam at this volume would have created a flood and mudflow two magnitudes greater than the May 18 eruption day mudflow down the North Fork Toutle River and the Main Branch Toutle, which had a peak discharge of 500,000 cubic feet per second (cfs) at its confluence with the Cowlitz River. The peak discharge after a breach of the Coldwater debris dam, and a 1:1 ratio mixing of lake water and debris avalanche sediments, would have been 960,000 cfs. The lake would have surely breached before reaching the top of the blockage, since the debris was so loosely consolidated and easily eroded.

The Coldwater Lake spillway was opened on July 8, 1981 and began drawing the lake down, easing the flood risk. The project was completed that September, with the bedrock portion of the spillway channel measuring 1300 feet long and 80 feet wide at its base. In addition to controlling the normal surface elevation of Coldwater Lake at 2471 feet in elevation, materials excavated from the spillway area were placed on the top of the embankment to provide additional crest width and freeboard.

Sandra Embrey and Norm Dion of the USGS performed water quality on Coldwater Lake from spring 1981 until August 1982. They reported the lake’s chemical analysis in USGS report 87-4263 (link at end of presentation) and measured the lake's clarity at a one-foot depth by Secchi-disk in May 1981.
A secchi-disk is a 20-centimeter diameter black and white target that is lowered into the water until it disappears from sight. The clarity had improved to 15 feet by August 1982, although the lake volume had increased substantially, and the floating log mat had been removed during construction of the outflow channel. A second bathymetric survey was completed in November 1981, when Coldwater Lake had a surface water level of 2471 feet, 188-foot depth, and 64,500-acre-foot volume.

Coldwater Lake bathymetry  November 1981  Water surface level 2471 feet   from USGS WRIR 4263

Scientists studying stream deposition of surge material into Coldwater Lake placed sediment traps just above the bottom and took a core sample in the lake’s deepest section (see link to Sedimentation in a Blast-Zone Lake at end of presentation). The sediment trap was left in place for one year until summer 1983 and was replaced and recovered again in 1984. Sediment recovered from the trap and from the core suggested deposition occurred six times as fast from dense material flowing along the bottom of the lake as from suspended sediment reaching the trap. Deposition into Coldwater Lake from its completely denuded basin was at least ten times greater than similar vegetated basins, and closely paralleled the year-to-year uniformity of varves (yearly deposit layers) in glacial lakes receiving great amounts of sediment. This bottom-flow deposition may explain a portion of the four-foot difference in the lake bottom elevation from the March 1981 bathymetric to the November 1981 bathymetric, along with possible underwater slumping of the debris avalanche plug. 
The Coldwater Lake outlet channel functioned properly and maintained the lake level at the planned surface elevation. Although extreme winter runoff was not experienced in the first three years after construction, the channel was capable of passing extreme runoff with only a minor temporary rise in the surface elevation of the lake. As the years passed, the lake was allowed to safely rise and has a present-day surface elevation of 2503 feet. The lake is 17,000 feet in length (3.2 miles) and covers a surface area of 751 acres. Coldwater Lake has lost some surface area over the years as two deltas formed at opposite ends of the lake, along with a large landslide debris fan. Coldwater Creek continues to build a delta at the head of the lake. The landslide fan is on Coldwater Lake’s northern shore, 3400 feet downstream from the head of the lake. At the opposite end, South Fork Coldwater Creek no longer flows into the lake where it previously built a large delta, but instead now joins Coldwater Lake’s outlet stream.
