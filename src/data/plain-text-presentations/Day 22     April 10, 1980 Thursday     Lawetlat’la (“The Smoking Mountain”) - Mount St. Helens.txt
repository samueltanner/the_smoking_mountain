The fabulous eight-photograph sequence that follows was taken by Austin Post and Bob Krimmel, glaciologists at the U.S. Geological Survey office in Tacoma. Austin and Bob took amazing and famous black and white oblique aerial photos (from an angle, not vertical). The first four photos that follow are some of my very favorites of the restless volcano. I have a lot of favorite photographs, but these are the real deal. The snow patterns on the volcano's southside "Worm Flows" are especially beautiful.
Whenever I was sent on an errand to Glaciology on the 8th floor, I loved to peek into various offices and see mountain climbing gear lying about...ropes, crampons, helmets, boots. Not the typical stuff one would have by your desk. Austin had a long beard, and looked like the wise old sage that he was. 
Colleen especially loved talking with Bob, since her father Bill was a professional photographer and had done lots of early aerial photography with the Army Air Corps and Air Force.
If you have ever read Fred Beckey's three "Cascade Alpine Guides", the classic Washington State mountaineering bibles, nearly every aerial was taken by Austin and Bob.

Day 22     April 10, 1980  Thursday
USGS daily report
Clouds covered the mountain most of the morning, but they cleared enough for aerial observation by early afternoon. Several major and minor eruptions of steam and ash occurred at irregular intervals, most of them lasting no more than a few minutes. A fresh snowfall last night gave residents and tourists to the south much the same view of the mountain that has existed for more than a century. Fracturing and slumping on the upper northern side, however, are causing cracks in the ice cap that are much too large to be hidden by the fresh snow. The progressively enlarging crater now measures about 2,000 feet by 1,200 feet. Coatings of gray volcanic ash give the northern side of the crater's interior a velvety look, except for some jagged rock outcroppings and scattered huge blocks of fallen rock on the crater floor. 
The eruptions are confined to a single circular vent, perhaps 30 feet across, in the position of the second (western) crater but now at least 500 feet lower than the opening originally blasted through the volcano's icecap on March 27th. The position of the first, more easterly crater has been largely obscured by crater growth. To the west of the active vent, however, the presence of a cone-shaped depression in the crater floor, separated from the active vent by a small, sharp ridge, suggests that a third orifice for the eruptions of steam and ash may have been active until recently. Some of the intermittent eruptions since March 27 have consisted of a single explosion; others have surged upward in pulses. The cloud sometimes consists of three parts: a lower, very dark, tephra-rich finger, an intermediate gray cloud of ash and steam, and an upper cloud of white steam. The intermediate part develops from the dark finger and soon roils out in all directions to obscure the finger that feeds it. Blocks of rock

and ice occasionally blast through the cloud. As the gray clouds drift downward, thin veils of ash fall from them. Lobate fingers of several "generations" of ash-darkened snow and ice avalanches reach down to the lower slopes of the mountain. The youngest are darkest and contrast sharply with the stark white of the newly fallen snow. Harmonic tremor, the first in three days, occurred twice today. One episode lasted for 22 minutes, the other for 16.
Gravity has been measured repeatedly at most of the established gravity stations, but, so far, measurements near the mountain have shown little change. Precise instrumental measurements on the mountain often are hampered by rapid, short-term tilting of the ground. Two scientists measuring the gravity at East Dome felt an earthquake and then watched with fascination as the level bubbles on their gravity instruments moved far off center in response to a strong upward tilt of the mountainside above them. This tilt was followed a few minutes later by a puff of steam from the crater, whereupon the bubbles registered a similar tilt in the opposite direction.

Historic photographs
It’s time to explore the other lakes around Mount St. Helens. I’ve spent a lot of time looking for pre-eruption photographs of the lakes near Mount St. Helens, but really haven’t found many save for Spirit Lake. In the next couple of days, I’ll share the photographs that I have found along with a few bathymetric maps (underwater contour) and snippets of topographic maps.
Bear Lake and Hoo Hoo Lake were located southeast of the mountain, on tributaries to Smith Creek and the Muddy River, respectively. Both lakes were small (<10 acres), and were rapidly becoming swamp [both lakes were inundated by the massive lahars on May 18, 1980 and no longer exist].
Fawn Lake is 9 miles NNW of the volcano. The Fawn Lake cirque was unlogged prior to the eruption, but roads were numerous right up to the outlet and everything else around the basin had been completely clear cut. [Fawn Lake was spectacularly affected by the May 18 eruption, and that’s a post-eruption story for later].
Hanaford, Elk, and Forest Lakes are also nine miles from Mount St. Helens, about a mile to the northeast of Fawn Lake. Hanaford Lake was also known as Frances Lake, Forest Lake as Doris. The Hanaford group had also been extensively logged, save for the areas immediately next to the lakes. The vertical aerial was taken in July 1968, and by 1980 the lake basins were a lot more chopped up. All four lakes drain northwesterly to Shultz Creek and the Green River.  Lying a mile+ north of the Hanaford group is Tradedollar Lake, eleven miles NNW of Mount St. Helens. Tradedollar Lake drains to Tradedollar Creek and the Green River. Tradedoller Lake had a bathymetric survey performed in early September 1954, like Elk and Hanaford Lakes. The maps were made by Lavier of the Washington State Department of Game. The USGS mapped Fawn Lake in September 1974.
