Day 76     June 3, 1980  Tuesday

USGS daily report
Harmonic tremor, which has been fluctuating in a range of low amplitudes for the past several days, fell off to a very low-level last night. Then, at about 0200 hours, it began to increase strongly. The University of Washington seismology center contacted USGS scientists in Vancouver and informed them that this pattern of harmonic tremor was similar to the one that had preceded the May 25th eruption. Furthermore, no earthquakes have occurred since Monday morning, reminiscent of the gradual decrease in earthquakes that took place before the May 18th eruption. Because of these similarities, scientists are keeping an especially careful watch on the volcano. As of midnight, however, no eruption is evident. 
The Corps of Engineers reports that its plans for flood control and water rehabilitation include not only dredging the rivers and raising dikes along the Cowlitz but also constructing rockfill dams and settling ponds on the two forks of the Toutle River. The dams and settling ponds are intended to catch additional sediment eroding from the area of the volcano blast and carried by the two river forks and lessen the filling of channels downstream. The captured sediment reportedly will be removed from catchment ponds later by earth-moving equipment. 
Automatic banking machines are the latest victims of the ash. Two bank machines in Yakima and 40 in Portland reportedly have been disabled by ash that has blown into their mechanisms. The ash may be useful in some manufacturing processes, however. A California firm announced that, when the ash is mixed with a plastic resin, it forms a porcelain like material that can be used to make bathroom fixtures and other products.   [USGS PP 1249]

Plinian columns are named after Roman Statesman Pliny the Younger, after describing the eruption of Mount Vesuvius in 79 A.D. Pliny the Younger traveled to Pompeii to discover the fate of his uncle, Pliny the Elder, who was asphyxiated while trying to rescue townspeople.
Mount St. Helens’ Plinian column began about four minutes after the May 18 landslide-induced 0832.20 eruption. As the debris avalanche exposed the edge of the cryptodome and released the lateral pyroclastic surge, the interior of the crater slumped downward and exposed to the sky the conduit supplying magma to the volcano. The column of hot, ash-charged gas rose to an altitude of 25,000 feet at 0838 hours, 35,000 feet at 0840, 59,000 feet at 0842. The column expanded rapidly into a mushroom shape and at 0845 the top of the ash plume had ascended to 75,000 feet. The airborne ash plume advanced rapidly to the northeast at an average velocity of 155 mph during the first 13 minutes after the eruption. The plume then traveled E-NE within a high-velocity wind layer at altitudes between 30-40,000 feet at an average velocity of 62 mph over the first 600 miles. By 0900 hours the ash plume had ascended perhaps as high as 88,000 feet. The ash plume passed over Yakima at 0945, 85 miles downwind. By 1012, the eruption column had subsided to about 43,000 feet, but continued unabated for 
Plinian Column on May 18 and Air-Fall Ash

nine hours. 
Lightning and other electrical phenomena associated with the eruption were spectacular. A heavy concentration of vertical lightning at altitudes of 25,000-30,000 feet was entirely within the vertical eruptive column. The were also many cloud-to-ground strikes, some of which started forest fires. Eyewitnesses stated that some of the lightning appeared red. Others observed the lightning wasn't normal. "First a white dot appeared in the cloud, and then a bolt would shoot out from it."
About 1215, the column grew much wider, paler gray, and far more turbulent. A pumiceous pyroclastic flow raced down the north flank across the debris avalanche. Pyroclastic flows continued down the volcano’s flanks throughout the afternoon, but diminished around 1630 hours. The Plinian column began to decrease in intensity at about 1730. A weak, ashy plume drifted east-northeast all night, and had decreased to a low level by the morning of May 19. The ash plume returned to the west coast of North America by early June after circling the globe. The finest ash remained suspended in the atmosphere and circled the earth many times. 

Four distinct layers of air-fall were deposited in Washington State areas east of the volcano.  The layered 
stratigraphy was composed of three, two, and one layer at progressively greater distances downwind.  In areas where two air-fall ash layers fell, a thin, dark layer represents tephra from Mount St. Helens’ summit area.  An upper, thicker, light grey ash layer is juvenile magma from the voluminous Plinian column. The volume of tephra and air-fall ash from the May 18 Plinian eruption column ranks 4th or 5th in Mount St. Helens’ history. If the Pyroclastic Density Current deposits are included, the 1980 eruption volume is ranked 3rd.
