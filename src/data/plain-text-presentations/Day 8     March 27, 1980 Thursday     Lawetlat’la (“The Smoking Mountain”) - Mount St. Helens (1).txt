Day 8     March 27, 1980  Thursday
USGS daily report
At 1120 hours, an observer in an Army National Guard reconnaissance airplane reported seeing a hole in the icecap on the mountain near the summit and a gray streak extending southeast from the hole. No emissions from the hole were reported at that time. Then, at 1236 hours, people near the volcano heard a loud boom. This boom probably marked the first eruption, which took place during cloudy weather. Mike Beard, a reporter from a Portland radio station, was flying over the cloud-shrouded mountain and apparently was the first to report seeing the eruption. He is quoted as saying that he "... saw ash and smoke spewing out, a little like smoke out of a chimney. It was not explosive.... " 
At 1400 hours, the University of Washington seismology center recorded a magnitude 4.7 earthquake, the second strongest to date. The quake was one of 57 during the day to register magnitude 3.5 or greater. Earthquakes have become so common in the Mount St. Helens area that a half dozen quakes greater than magnitude 4 are considered normal for a day. After this magnitude 4.7 shock, the eruption, as described by airborne observers, was a thick black plume rising about 7,000 feet above the volcano. Observers flying over the mountain shortly afterward saw a crater about 200 to 250 feet wide near the summit. They also noted that the ice, snow, and rocks around the crater were deformed and that volcanic ash had darkened the snow in a band that extended across the crater and down the cone's southeastern slope. Two crack systems trending east-west were seen extending across the peak. The southernmost crack is about 1 mile long; it turns north on both the eastern and western sides of the summit and reaches short distances down the northeastern and northwestern flanks.

Within hours of the initial eruption, hundreds of people were evacuated from scattered homes, Forest Service stations, fish hatcheries, and three Weyerhaeuser Company logging camps near the volcano. Sheriff's deputies set up roadblocks on several main routes to keep out the curious. One longtime resident, Harry Truman, proprietor of the Mount St. Helens Lodge at the southwestern end of Spirit Lake, refused to leave despite strong urging to do so.   [USGS PP 1249]

Before we dive any further into the reawakening of Mount St. Helens, the research by U.S. Geological Survey geologists Dwight (Rocky) Crandell and Donal Mullineaux is fascinating and is definitely required reading.  
Crandell and Mullineaux began surveying the Cascade volcanoes from Mount Baker to Mount Shasta and Lassen Peak in the 1950s. The two geologists and Jack Hyde took special interest in Mount St. Helens for nearly two decades (1960-1978), studying ash layers and mudflows and charred trees dated by the new carbon-dating method. They discovered that Mount St. Helens was the violent volcano, compared with the nearby giants Mounts Rainier and Adams. They concluded that "the volcano's behavior pattern suggests that the current quiet interval will not last as long as a thousand years; instead, an eruption is more likely to occur within the next hundred years, and perhaps even before the end of this century." The geologists basically predicted that Lawetlat’la was likely to erupt before the year 2000. Predictions of this type are unheard of in the field of geology and volcanology, where time is normally expressed in thousands and millions of years, not thirty to forty. They received a lot of unwanted notoriety about their "prediction" and always tried to deflect any reporter's repeated questions back to “today” on the mountain.
Colleen and I loved that the copy of Future Eruptions of Mount St. Helens on the next page belonged to Carolyn Driedger. Carolyn was well liked and respected at the Tacoma USGS office. Carolyn researched the Shoestring Glacier from 1979 on, and studied the effects of varying ash thicknesses on snow and ice and resulting ablation rates. She worked upstairs in Glaciology, and moved to the Vancouver Cascades Volcano observatory after the eruption. Carolyn was supposed to stay overnight in the trailer at Coldwater II with fellow volcanologists Melinda Brugman and David Johnston, but the two women were pulled away, back to headquarters in the early evening of May 17. If you visit the Johnston Ridge Observatory, you can watch Carolyn talking volcanoes on the big screen.

Before continuing with much more recent events on Mount St. Helens’ flanks, an abbreviated timeline of the volcano’s eruptive history…
• Mount St. Helens is about 40,000 years old, created during the Ape Canyon eruptive stage 50,000 to 36,000 years ago. 
• The Cougar (20,000 to 18,000 years ago) and Swift Creek (13,000 to 10,000) eruptive stages followed, along with a dormant interval lasting 5000+ years.
• The volcano is currently in the Spirit Lake eruptive stage (3900 years ago to present)...

Within the Spirit Lake stage, the Smith Creek eruptive period (3900 to 3300) included the largest eruption in Mount St. Helen’s history. The giant eruption occurred about 3510 years ago, comparable in size to Mount Vesuvius in 79 A.D. and thirteen times larger than Mount St. Helen’s 1980 blast. One cubic mile of tephra was deposited to the northeast across Washington State and into Canada as Layer Yn. The ancestral Spirit Lake was formed during the Smith Creek period. 
• The Pine Creek eruptive period was 2900 to 2500 years ago. The period included a breach of Spirit Lake,  generating a massive North Fork toutle River lahar that blocked a tributary creek and created Silver Lake.
• The Castle Creek period (2200 to 1600 years ago)  The Dog’s Head dacite dome was formed. About 1900 years ago, basaltic lava flows created the Ape Caves and Lava Canyon on the southeast flank. By the end of the Castle Creek period, Mount St. Helen’s summit had nearly reached it’s 1980 height.  
• The Sugar Bowl period (age range uncertain)  The Sugar Bowl dacite dome was created, and likely the East Dome. A lateral pyroclastic surge extended 18 miles to the northwest, seven miles wide, estimated at 1150 years 

ago by radiocarbon dating. 
• The Kalama eruptive period (1480 A.D. to late 1700s)  The 1480 eruption produced the Wn tephra layer deposited in the Cispus and Cowlitz River drainages. The eruption was dated by tree ring analysis. 
• The Goat Rocks period (1800 to 1857 A.D.)  Commencing in 1800, eruptions deposited the T tephra layer to the northeast and the Floating Island andesitic lava flow to the northwest. Eruptions in the Goat Rocks period were also dated by tree ring analysis. The Goat Rocks dacite dome was constructed from 1831 to 1857. 
• A 123-year dormant period followed…
