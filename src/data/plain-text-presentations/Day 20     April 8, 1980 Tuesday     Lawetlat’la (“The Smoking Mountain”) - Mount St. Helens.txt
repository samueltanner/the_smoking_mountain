April 8, 1980 Erupted plumes of ash and steam were carried from the crater by winds and sometimes streamed down the flanks of Mount St. Helens for considerable distances. Spectacular displays of lightning occasionally were generated in these ground-hugging plumes by charges of static electricity built up when ash particles rubbed against one another in the turbulent cloud. Day 20     April 8, 1980  Tuesday
USGS daily report
One long, single eruption, visible for more than 5 hours, was first seen at 0822 hours and continued until at least 1400 hours today. During the eruption, a hole opened in the ice at the head of Wishbone Glacier, near the northwestern rim of the crater, and melt water from the surrounding ice poured into the hole for several minutes to form a pond. Then the rim cracked, the water disappeared, and a slice of the upper Wishbone Glacier caved into the crater, enlarging the crater's northwestern side by more than 300 feet. [The Wishbone Glacier was the second largest pre-eruption, slightly smaller than the Forsyth Glacier on the northeast flank]. For several minutes after the cave-in, airborne observers noted an especially heavy concentration of dark ash in the eruption cloud. On calm days, the white clouds of steam from the volcano sometimes rise nearly 10,000 feet above the crater rim, and they often resemble thunderheads. On windy days, the steam is carried out laterally from the crater. At times, winds direct the clouds downslope on the leeside of the cone as a ground-hugging plume that deposits ash as it moves. A pattern of black scallops on the flank of the volcano often marks the lower limit of these downslope plumes. There is still much uncertainty and speculation about what the volcano will do next. Some news reporters complain that they get a different idea or view from each scientist interviewed, depending on his background and specialty. Most scientists prefer to withhold judgment until they have more data to help them decipher the volcanic events.   [USGS PP 1249]

Historic photographs
More photographs and big views from the Mount Margaret Backcountry across Spirit Lake to Mount St. Helens. The flowers and tall grass are back, and the Backcountry is once again a very green world 45 years after the eruption. Late summer walking can be super slow, with bushes full of plump huckleberries every shade from blue to purple to almost black.
