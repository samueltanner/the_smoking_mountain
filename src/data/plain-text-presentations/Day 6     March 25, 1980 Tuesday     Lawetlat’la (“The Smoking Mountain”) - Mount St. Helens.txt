Day 6     March 25, 1980  Tuesday

USGS daily report  
Seismic activity increased greatly again on March 25. Five earthquakes of magnitude 4 or greater were recorded in one hour (22 earthquakes during an 8-hour period). At this rate, recorder traces of earthquakes even as large as magnitude 3.5 were buried in the continuations of traces of previous quakes. At noon, the paper went dark with overlying earthquake traces. 
The Forest Service closed areas on the mountain above timberline, and the Federal Aviation Administration imposed special flight restrictions near the volcano because of the number of aircraft carrying scientists, reporters, and other observers for a close look. 
Bud Kimball, a commercial aerial photographer, reported to USGS scientists in Tacoma that he had photographed a large crack in the snow and ice across the top of Mount St. Helens.   [USGS PP 1249]

Historic paintings 
In the Spring of 2020, as the lockdown commenced, Sandy Embrey told me about wanting to see The Portland Art Museum’s 40th-anniversary Exhibition, Volcano! Mount St. Helens in Art but knew she wasn’t going to be able to go. It would have been fun to see, but the Museum shut down and the plans erupted. You will meet Sandy in many of the post-eruption stories. The museum’s neat link to the artwork sadly disappeared one day. Luckily, I had saved several of the images. Then the old link was back up and running! It’s great. 
Volcano! Mount St. Helens in Art
Darn, now the Portland Museum link is gone again in March 2024. I’ll leave the link here with crossed fingers.

Here are a dozen of the old paintings, ten painted between 1845 and 1894 during the Goat Rocks eruptive period (165-190 years ago). The first recorded eruption from the volcanic plug was in 1835. Major eruptions occurred during the years 1842-44, with sporadic activity lasting until 1857. 
Funny that the European artists in the bunch seemed to try and make everything Matterhornesque during that time. The first paintings by Henry James Warre and especially the eighth by Albert Bierstadt look more like Mount Hood to me. Mount Adams is right behind though in Bierstadt’s depiction, exactly where it should be. Some very striking post-eruption paintings and media will be shared later.
Small eruptions of Mount St. Helens were witnessed in 1898, 1903 and 1921, but the volcano was only beginning a very short slumber. Mount St. Helens was as large or larger than at any time in its history as the 20th century began.
